# 3. HALLOWMOOR

> Seed: `blight` · Goal fit **34/40** · Distinctiveness **4/5** · Feasibility **2/5** · Verdict: **promising**

**Pitch.** Hallowmoor is a co-op survival-automation game where a handful of lamplighters hold the last parish on a blighted frontier. You build your factory out of the dead — bone mills, soul-fired kilns, processions of bound skeletons hauling along lantern-lit lych-roads — while a living blight-tide probes your borders, studies your defenses, and evolves counters. Every soul you harvest is fuel, labor, defense, or permanent advancement: never all four, and the night is always coming.

**The hook.** A factory game where the dead are simultaneously your ore, your engines, your conveyor belts, and your enemy — and the blight studies how you use them and evolves to take them back, while your base is judged by how peacefully its dead sleep.

**Tone.** Dark pastoral: warm lantern-gold pooled against desaturated moss, slate, and the bruised violet of blightland — Over the Garden Wall meets Darkest Dungeon in a Bruegel village. Hand-painted isometric 2D with chunky readable silhouettes and dynamic 2D lighting as the single big art-budget bet, because light literally is the power grid, the defense line, and the mood; entirely plausible for a small team shipping WebGL/canvas to browsers.

## How it serves the founding goals

### Goal 1 — constant pressure

The pressure source is the Blight Tide: a continuously simulated scalar field on the tile grid that creeps toward consecrated land, surges every night (the Gloaming), and spikes hard on semi-random Rot Moons every few in-game days. Inside its reach: miasma drains player Resolve, spores corrupt grave-barley fields, and — the key screw — any corpse left unprocessed on either side of your wall rises. Your own kills are a ticking liability, so combat victories create urgent logistics work, not rest. Three scaling mechanisms keep it permanent: (1) perimeter scaling — every tile you purify lengthens the border you must keep lit and blessed, so expansion inherently buys more pressure; (2) upkeep decay — consecration fades without Lumen from the lantern grid, and lanterns burn grave-oil, so your safety is a flow, never a stockpile; (3) the Grudge — the blight accrues adaptation against whatever is killing it most (fire, salt, bell-tone, blade) and spawns resistant strains led by named Heralds. It never becomes a chore because the tide is spatial and behavioral rather than a bigger HP bar: it probes your dimmest wall segment, flows around obstacles, exploits unlit corridors and dry riverbeds, and its evolved strains change WHAT kind of problem the next surge is (diggers under walls, lantern-snuffing wisps, corpse-thieves that steal your ore). Late game you are not grinding bigger walls; you are managing an opponent that answers your last three decisions.

### Goal 2 — the automation layers

Tier-0 manual tasks: swinging a censer tile-by-tile to purify ground, hauling corpses to the pyre, hand-rendering corpses into bone/tallow/marrow, hand-cranking the bone mill, refilling every lantern each dusk, sowing and cutting grave-barley. The automation metaphor: the dead labor for you. Layers: (1) BOUND DEAD — soul-embers bound into skeleton laborers who haul, harvest, and stoke along player-drawn Procession routes; replaces manual hauling and farming, but they need consecrated ground or go feral. (2) LYCH-ROADS AND CORPSE-CARTS — paved bone roads with ghoul-drawn carts, the belt/train analog; replaces long-distance carrying and enables outposts. (3) SOUL-FIRED WORKS — the Rendery (auto-processes corpses into bone, tallow, marrow), Bone Mill, Tallow Press, and Kiln, chained by carts; replaces hand-processing, burns embers as fuel. (4) THE LANTERN GRID AND BELL TOWERS — reliquary shrines (relic = generator core) push Lumen down lines of lantern posts; bell towers on a crafted hymn-schedule re-bless tiles in radius, replacing manual censer upkeep — the power grid and the territory-maintenance system are the same buildout. (5) WARDWORKS — salt-caster turrets, blessed-oil sprayers, patrolling gargoyles automating border defense. (6) GHOST COURIERS — late-game spectral logistics that move small high-value goods between districts without roads. Why it is fresh versus Factorio belts: your throughput units are agents drawn from the same corpse economy as your fuel, your building material, and your enemies — one resource (the dead) is simultaneously ore, engine, workforce, and threat — and your power layout is your defense layout, so factory routing and fortification are one act, not two screens.

### Goal 2 — the sacrifice economy

The atom of sacrifice is the soul-ember. Each ember can be (1) burned as machine fuel, (2) bound into a bone-servant for permanent labor, (3) sealed into a ward-lantern for defense, or (4) laid to rest at the Requiem altar — destroyed forever in exchange for Grace, the advancement currency. Advancement literally consumes the resource your survival loop runs on tonight. Secondary sacrifices: relics are finite and exploration-gated, so consecrating a new district can mean deconsecrating an old one; conscripting your farm skeletons as battle thralls trades this week's throughput for tonight's fight; and you can deliberately cede a district — retreat the lanterns, let it drown — to shorten your perimeter, with the twist that fallowed blightland grows blightglass and black marrow you can reclaim moons later, making retreat an investment rather than pure loss. Worked example, minute 30: six corpses from your first shambler wave, the Gloaming four minutes out. Each corpse can be pyred now (one ember each; your two lanterns need two embers to survive the night), hand-rendered (two minutes each) for the bone your first mill needs, or left — and anything unburned at nightfall rises inside your wall. You have time for three. And do you spend one ember binding your first bone-servant — useless tonight, hauling forever after — leaving the second lantern dark and a gap you must plug with your own censer and body? Worked example, hour 20: Rot Moon in two days; the Grudge shows 60% fire resistance, so your pyre-turret line is about to fail. Options: lay to rest the thirty souls in the western reliquary to unlock the Choir of the Third Bell (auto-reconsecration freeing two players' worth of nightly upkeep) but dim the west wall through the Moon; cede the western district entirely — perimeter shrinks 40%, but the marrowwood grove feeding four renderies goes silent and blightglass is two moons away; or hold everything and convert turrets from fire to salt, spending the consecrated silver you were hoarding for a ghost-courier hub. All three are viable and each commits your next ten hours differently.

### Goal 4 — the map changes the plan

Procedural variables: ley-line layout (where consecration is cheap and shrines are strong), rivers (the tide cannot cross running water), barrow density (soul and bone richness), ruin placement (which relics exist and where), marsh and peat fields (miasma but free fuel), the number and bearing of blight fronts, and which two or three artisan survivors your starting hamlet includes. Concrete reactions: (1) the map deals a river bisecting the region, so my strategy becomes a canal-fortress — fight only at two bridgeheads, spend almost nothing on turrets, dig a channel to close the one dry gap, and go industry-heavy behind the water; (2) the ley-lines cluster deep inside blighted land, so turtling is off the table — I run lantern-trains, mobile processions consecrating a corridor to a distant strongpoint, and my base becomes a string of pearls rather than a ring; (3) the map is barrow-poor, so souls are scarce and I pivot to ranching the dead — building kill-corridors that farm the tide itself as my primary ore vein, deliberately keeping a front active where a richer map would have sealed it.

### Goal 5 — base ⇄ combat loop

Base to combat: (1) the Chandlery and Saltworks produce your consumable ammunition — candle-bombs, salt cartridges, blessed-oil flasks — so weapon uptime is a direct function of your tallow and salt production chains; (2) players regenerate Resolve (the stamina/sanity shield that gates dodges and heavy swings) only inside lantern light, so pushing a lantern line toward a barrow is literally extending your combat power projection, and expeditions begin by building a forward lamppost; (3) bone-lamination armor and bell-toned blades require mill-kiln-press chains you cannot hand-crank at scale. Combat and exploration to base: (1) saints' relics looted from barrows and drowned chapels are unique generator cores, each enabling a machine behavior — St. Maren's Finger lets bell towers chain-ring, the Drowned Psalter lets lanterns burn underwater for river defenses; (2) killing a Herald of an evolved strain yields its ichor, which crafts the counter-ward to that adaptation AND teaches a grafted recipe reverse-engineered from it (snuffer-wisp ichor unlocks shadow-shutter lamps; digger ichor unlocks root-pile foundations); (3) survivors rescued from ruins become named artisan NPCs — the knacker, the vicar, the cooper — who staff buildings, raise throughput, and carry unique recipes home. The loop closes tightly: fighting produces corpses, corpses are ore, ore becomes the machines that make the next fight winnable.

### Goals 3 + 6 — many paths, no milestones

There is no research tree. Three order-free systems replace it: (1) RELICS — unlocks come from which unique relics your particular map contains and which you choose to go get, so capability order is map-authored and route-authored, not menu-authored; (2) COVENANTS — Grace is spent at the Requiem altar on a web of saints' covenants with no prerequisites, including mutually exclusive vows (the Vow of Poverty makes all machines cheaper but caps stockpiles; the Vow of Silence disables bells but doubles lantern radius) that push builds apart; (3) GRAFTS — recipes harvested from Herald ichor, meaning the blight's adaptation to YOUR playstyle generates your unlock path — the antagonist co-authors your tech. Progression state is simply the world: front position, strains evolved, relics held, souls resting, villagers rescued. Genuinely different archetypes that fall out: the Bastion Parish (deep turtle on a chokepoint, dense vertical industry, bell automation, farms the tide for corpses); the Procession (semi-nomadic lantern-train chasing ley-lines and barrows, cedes land behind it, ghost-courier logistics, almost no walls); the Black Shepherds (deliberately cultivate fallowed blight pastures to grow blightglass and bait specific strains for ichor, skirmish-heavy, graft-tech economy, minimal consecrated core).

### Goal 7 — beauty pays rent

Solace: the dead rest easier in beautiful ground. Tiles accrue Solace from the same objects that do functional work — overlapping lantern light forming symmetric vesper-lines, bell towers spaced at harmonic intervals with unobstructed tone, graveyards in aligned rows with planted borders, processional lych-roads that run straight, lit, and colonnaded. Solace multiplies Grace generated per soul laid to rest, speeds artisan NPCs, and raises player Resolve regen; bone-servants walk measurably faster along dignified processions. The stick is native to the fiction: a cramped, dark, cacophonous industrial block still functions but bleeds Solace, and districts below threshold turn your own laid-to-rest dead restless — ugly bases literally get haunted by their graveyards, spawning wisps inside the walls. It produces builds that are both functional and lovely because every input to beauty IS infrastructure: you cannot decorate your way to Solace with inert props, you can only route light, sound, roads, graves, and gardens gracefully — and because light coverage, bell radius, and road efficiency are also your grid, upkeep, and logistics, the beautiful arrangement and the working arrangement are the same drawing. Feedback is a soft additive glow shader, so beauty is read directly off the screen, not off a meter.

### Goal 8 — why you're doing this

Outer frame: you are the Lamplighters of the last parish on the Hallowmoor, and the goal is to rekindle the chain of Great Beacons — cathedral-scale lights, procedurally placed, reachable in any order — whose burning once held the whole frontier, and ultimately to reach the First Grave at the blight's heart and give the unquiet kingdom rest (a server epilogue choice: extinguish the blight, or lay the kingdom down; play continues after). Not a quest chain — beacons are physical logistics problems, each demanding a different sustained supply line (one needs 40 grave-oil a minute for an hour-long ignition ritual; one stands mid-river; one is inside a Herald's territory), and each lit beacon permanently pushes the regional tide baseline back, reveals ley-lines, draws survivor caravans, and raises the Grudge. The cascade from narrative to moment-to-moment: (1) the Vigil — lanterns must be fueled before every dusk, so the next ten minutes always have a reason; (2) the Ledger of the Dead — a parish register of named souls not yet at rest, each a small findable, buriable, Grace-yielding objective, so the mid-loop always has a reason; (3) beacon logistics — the factory exists to feed an ignition, so the build order always has a reason; (4) rescued survivors and caravans tie every expedition back to a face at home. At any moment the chain reads: fuel tonight's lanterns, so the lych-road reaches St. Alder's Beacon, so the moor is lit end to end.

## Multiplayer (2–20, persistent)

One parish per persistent server, 2-20 players. Division of labor falls out of the systems rather than classes: Lamplighter (border patrol and refueling), Knacker (corpse logistics and rendering chains), Vicar (consecration, bells, graveyard and Solace design), Warden (expeditions and Herald hunts), Artificer (machine chains and covenant planning) — and land and relics are communal while Grace and covenant vows are personal, which generates real negotiation (the group must decide whose vow shapes the shared factory). Pressure scales with player count diegetically: the tide hungers toward the living, so aggression scales with active players plus total Grace earned, and perimeter scaling naturally follows group ambition. Offline, the parish holds Vigil: bound dead keep working their processions, artisan NPCs keep core lanterns lit at inflated oil cost, and the front creeps only at the fringes — server settings choose between full pause and slow-burn for small groups. Player death is multiplayer-native: your body lies where you fell and you play on as an unquiet shade (weakened, unable to touch relics) until teammates recover and properly bury you — and where they bury you matters, because your grave contributes Solace like any other.

## Closest existing games

- Factorio — logistics are corpse-agents, enemy adapts, territory needs upkeep
- They Are Billions — persistent co-op tide, factory depth, no RTS micro
- Graveyard Keeper — corpse economy played for dread, real threat, multiplayer
- Against the Storm — one persistent adapting world, not roguelite runs
- Valheim — top-down browser co-op, automation core replaces boss-gated tiers

## Designer-admitted risks

- Server cost and netcode: a continuously simulated blight field plus dozens of pathing bone-servant agents on cheap fly.io instances for 20 browser clients is a genuine engineering problem; needs hierarchical chunked simulation, road-constrained agent pathing, and aggressive interest management, and the design dies if the tide must tick too coarsely to feel alive.
- The adaptive Grudge is a knife-edge: too weak and players find one unanswerable defense and the game is solved; too strong and it reads as rubber-band punishment that taxes whatever the player enjoys. Making adaptation feel like a dialogue with counterplay (telegraphed strains, hunt-able Heralds) rather than a cheat is the hardest tuning problem in the design.
- Upkeep-heavy pressure (oil, consecration decay, corpse processing) can curdle from tension into chore treadmill, especially for solo players and small groups returning to a decayed persistent world; the offline Vigil rules will make or break retention and there is no proven template for hard survival pressure in a persistent co-op world at this scale.
- Solace is at risk of collapsing into one optimal lattice players stamp everywhere, which would fail goal 7 exactly the way bolted-on beauty meters do; it only works if map-specific inputs (ley-lines, rivers, terrain) shift the beautiful optimum per world, and that interaction is unproven and hard to make legible without turning the screen into overlay soup.
- The sacrifice economy invites death spirals: losing a district cuts production, which cuts defense, which loses the next district. Ceded-land payoffs (blightglass, shorter perimeter) must be strong enough that retreat is a strategy rather than a slow loss animation, and tuning comeback strength without making land loss consequence-free is a narrow target.

**Biggest weakness.** The design demands constant, hard, adaptive pressure inside a persistent 2-20 player world, and those two commitments are structurally opposed: any offline rule strong enough to protect absent players neuters "the night is always coming," while any rule weak enough to preserve it turns returning players into decay janitors. Every other system — sacrifice tension, Solace, beacon logistics — hangs off pressure feeling fair across asynchronous schedules, and the pitch defers the contradiction to "server settings," which is where designs go to admit they have no answer.

**How a real server dies (failure scenario).** Day 10 on a four-friend server: two players lapse for a week. The remaining pair logs in Sunday to a world tuned for four — perimeter length, Grudge level, and tide aggression were all ratcheted up by the group's lifetime Grace and expansion, none of which shrank when half the labor left. Slow-burn Vigil has ceded the western district, the marrowwood renderies are silent, and the fire-resistant strain that evolved against their absent Warden's turret line is at 60%. Their entire three-hour session is triage: refueling lanterns, hand-processing the corpses of their own risen bone-servants, and re-consecrating decayed tiles — they never place one new machine, never make one interesting sacrifice, because every ember must go to lanterns. The comeback lever (ceded land yields blightglass "two moons later") sits beyond any session horizon they can see. They cede two more districts to shorten the wall, recognize they are watching a slow loss animation, and the server goes dark — the exact death spiral the pitch lists as risk five, arriving via the exact retention gap it lists as risk three.

**The steelman fix.** Design the persistence model first, not last: let the parish be deliberately banked into a dormant Hearth-Vigil — perimeter auto-contracts to a sanctified core, the tide fully freezes, the Grudge slowly decays — whenever active players drop below a threshold, and key all pressure scaling (aggression, perimeter cost, strain level) off currently-awake territory rather than lifetime Grace and expansion. This extends the pitch's own best idea, retreat-as-investment, into the time dimension, so absence becomes a strategic contraction instead of decay, and it converts the design's most likely killer (asynchronous co-op schedules) into another sacrifice decision.

**Distinctiveness (4/5).** Closest game: The Riftbreaker. The minute-to-minute space — build a factory whose power grid is your defense line against an evolving alien tide — is already shipped in The Riftbreaker (which the pitch conspicuously omits from its comparables), but the corpse-unified economy, persistent co-op parish, sacrifice-based advancement, and Solace are real differentiators IF the simulation ships at fidelity; the fiction alone would not be enough.

**Feasibility (2/5).** The expensive part is server-authoritative RTS-scale simulation on budget hardware: a continuously ticking blight scalar field with flow/probe behavior, plus dozens-to-hundreds of pathing skeleton agents, plus adaptive strain AI, replicated in real time to 20 browser clients on fly.io-class instances — and the pitch itself concedes the design dies if the tick coarsens. Second, underestimated cost: hand-painted isometric art with dynamic 2D lighting across a very large roster of buildings, machines, strains, Heralds, and NPCs is not a small-team art budget, 'single big bet' framing notwithstanding. This is two hard games (Factorio-class logistics sim and They Are Billions-class tide sim) welded together and shipped to browsers.
