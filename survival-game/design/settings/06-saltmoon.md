# 6. SALTMOON

> Seed: `tide` · Goal fit **33/40** · Distinctiveness **4/5** · Feasibility **2/5** · Verdict: **promising**

**Pitch.** A co-op survival-automation game on a world that drowns and drains every 40 real minutes, on a clock every player on the server shares. You build tide-powered factories out of terraces, sluices, and locks that harvest the exposed seabed at ebb and survive the flood at high water — while the falling moon raises the tides a little more every week, and the drowned engines that could tame them lie in the deepest seabeds, exposed only minutes at a time.

**The hook.** A factory game where the conveyor belt is the tide itself: the whole map floods and drains on a real-time clock every player shares, and everything you build has to work twice — drowned and drained.

**Tone.** Hand-painted isometric 2D whose palette swaps with the tide: glistening mud, salt-crust whites, and long shadows at ebb; ink-dark water, bioluminescent predator wakes, and lantern-lit terraces at flood — Moebius meets Dutch delta engineering, with one rendering splurge (a good animated waterline shader over cheap tilemaps). Emotionally it alternates deadline dread ("the water is coming back") with tide-mill coziness when your locks clack and your pans steam through a flood you've earned.

## How it serves the founding goals

### Goal 1 — constant pressure

Three stacked sources. (1) THE TIDE ITSELF: every ~40 minutes the map floods; unsealed machines drown, loose goods wash away, low routes close. It is a deadline, not attrition — each ebb is a timed window you must budget. (2) SALT AND CORROSION: anything wetted fouls and corrodes; unattended, machines seize. Countered by sacrificial zinc anodes and mosaic cladding — upkeep you engineer away, never delete. (3) MOONSPAWN PREDATORS: sirenfish swarms and shellback reavers ride the flood in, homing on your base's "wake" (heat, light, turbulence) — pressure that scales with your own industry, like Factorio pollution but sensory and per-cycle. Long-term scaling: the moon is visibly falling, so tidal amplitude grows every server-week — spring highs bite higher (your safe footprint shrinks; last month's seawall overtops) while spring lows expose deeper, richer seabed (the carrot and the stick are the same water level). It never becomes a chore because the pressure is rhythmic and anticipatable: the almanac tells you exactly when the next spring tide hits, so play is a rolling series of plans and gambles ("18 minutes of ebb — trench run or wall repair?") rather than constant noise.

### Goal 2 — the automation layers

The automation metaphor: THE TIDE IS THE MACHINE. Water is your belt, your power source, and your enemy, and the whole logistics layer is pulsed and bidirectional instead of continuous — fresh versus Factorio because throughput has PHASE: goods and energy arrive twice a cycle, in alternating directions, and your factory must breathe with it. Tier-0 manual tasks: prying nodules and shellfish with a hand pick at ebb, hauling baskets uphill before the flood, bailing the cellar, hand-cranking the desalinator bellows, standing spear-watch at night flood, opening sluice gates by hand. Layers: (1) WEIRS & PANS — fish weirs, oyster racks, kelp lines, salt evaporation pans planted in the intertidal; the tide harvests for you (kills foraging). (2) SLUICE LOGISTICS — canals, lock gates, tethered buoy-crates: goods ride the flood uphill and the ebb downhill; elevation and gate timing ARE your routing logic (kills hauling). (3) TIDAL POWER — flood turbines in channels, rising-water air-compression bells, high cisterns that fill at flood and power machines through the ebb; energy must be buffered or scheduled (kills hand-cranking). (4) PROCESSING — brine boilers, nodule crackers, smokehouses, moonglass kilns fed by sluices (kills hand-crafting). (5) BASE SURVIVABILITY — float-valve seawalls, auto-pumps, anode racks, lure-beacons and tension harpoon batteries that hold the flood while you're offline (kills bailing and spear-watch). (6) DEEP OPERATIONS — seabed causeways, rail-sledges, dive bells, and dredges that only run during spring lows (kills the brutal deep hauls).

### Goal 2 — the sacrifice economy

Four currencies of sacrifice. MATERIALS ARE DUAL-USE: zinc is sacrificial anodes (survival upkeep) or gate valves and cells (automation); wood is desalinator fuel or sluice gates; smoked fish feeds you or your haul-beasts. WINDOW TIME: intertidal construction is only possible at low tide, so every minute spent building a canal is a minute of exposed seabed you didn't harvest — automation literally costs harvest. SAFETY: every machine adds wake, drawing heavier flood predators; industrializing paints a target on your base. ELEVATION REAL ESTATE: the safe hilltop footprint (shrinking as the moon falls) must hold your bed, stockpile, cistern, AND flywheel tower — something gets evicted to the flood zone. Minute-30 decision: one copper ingot, four minutes of ebb left, 30% thirst. Forge a copper still-pot (fresh water stops being a hand-chore tonight) or fittings for your first fish weir — which must be planted in the intertidal NOW or you wait a full cycle, and the incoming flood might catch you placing it. Hour-20 decision: a spring low in 15 minutes exposes the tin trench for ~6 minutes, once every 6 hours. Your bronze covers EITHER a compressed-air bell letting two players work the whole window, OR the float-valve seawall segment without which tonight's spring HIGH — now higher than your wall, thanks to moonfall — drowns the smeltery cellar and your power buffer. You cannot build both. Do you gamble the smeltery on whoever's online manually pumping?

### Goal 4 — the map changes the plan

The generator varies the elevation histogram (how much land exists at each tide band), highland shape (one mesa vs. an archipelago), seabed composition (kelp forest, ooze flat, nodule field, trench placement), freshwater seeps, and channel topology — where water rips through fastest is both the best turbine site and the predator highway. Examples: (1) Dealt a single sheer mesa with no intertidal shelf: safe base but starving flats — I go vertical, building crane-lifts and a 30-meter lock-staircase, feed on deep traps, and overinvest in sluice logistics. (2) Dealt a vast flat intertidal plain: enormous ebb harvests but nowhere dry at spring high — I hunt shellbacks early for keel-plates and go buoyant, a raft-base that rides the flood, accepting weak smelting and trading ore-for-food with the mesa clan. (3) Dealt a narrow strait between two highlands: free premium turbine power in the rip current, but the strait is THE predator funnel — I build power-rich and militarized, harpoon batteries before luxuries, and export compressed air.

### Goal 5 — base ⇄ combat loop

Base → combat: (1) the tide-forge and moonglass kiln cast harpoon heads and pressure-quenched brinesteel — weapon quality is downstream of base industry, and moonglass tempered by quenching at different tide phases has different properties. (2) Compressed-air infrastructure fills dive bells and air bladders, letting you fight and work UNDERWATER during flood — base investment flips the dangerous phase into an operating phase. (3) The lure-beacon network lets you choose where flood predators make landfall, turning defense into farming: bait them down a kill-canal into harpoon batteries. Combat/exploration → base: (1) shellback reavers drop keel-plates, the only buoyant hull material — floating base modules and barges are gated on hunting, not research. (2) Deep-ruin dives recover intact Tidewright mechanisms (a working float-valve brain, a lock-gate cam) that you reverse-engineer into new buildables — machine unlocks come home from the world. (3) Killing the moray tyrant nesting in a trench permanently clears a dredge site and yields the sinew for tension harpoon batteries, your best offline defense.

### Goals 3 + 6 — many paths, no milestones

There is no tech tree; three continuous systems replace it. (1) THE ELEVATION-TIME ENVELOPE: better materials sit deeper, and deeper seabed is exposed more rarely and more briefly. Nothing is locked — a day-1 player can sprint to the tin trench on a lucky spring low; they just can't carry much, survive the return, or smelt the take. Progression is capability, not permission, and it advances continuously (a better wall, a longer air supply, a faster sledge each independently extend your envelope). (2) ARTIFACT RECIPES: machine knowledge comes from recovered Tidewright mechanisms scattered semi-randomly, so which automations you get early depends on which ruins your map rolled and which you dove — two servers have different mid-games. (3) COMBINATORIAL CRAFT: alloy ratios and tide-phase quenching make gear a gradient with many local optima, not rungs. Distinct viable archetypes: Mesa Industrialists (fixed high base, lock-canals, walls, heavy smelting); Tidechasers (buoyant nomads chasing spring lows around the map, barge-mounted gear, harvest-and-run, combat-heavy); Slackwater Farmers (capture a tide-engine early, damp a lagoon into a safe but metal-poor aquaculture machine, and trade); Bell Cultists (compressed-air everything, live sealed BELOW the low-tide line and invert the cycle, working during flood).

### Goal 7 — beauty pays rent

Water rewards grace, natively. The coarse flow model penalizes turbulence: sharp corners, dead-end plumbing, and abrupt drops cause silt deposition, barnacle fouling, and bank erosion — maintenance chores — while smooth curves, stepped terraces, and balanced lock pairs run clean and fast. So the efficient factory converges on what humans already read as beautiful: rice-terrace contours, symmetric lock staircases, gentle meanders. Second prong: predators hunt by WAKE, and turbulent, chaotic plumbing has a loud signature — a laminar base is literally a stealthy base, so elegance is defense. Third prong: the anti-fouling, anti-corrosion coating is crushed-moonglass-and-shell mosaic, hand-set on wetted surfaces — the glittering tiled cladding on your walls and channels isn't decor, it's the maintenance layer, and a fully mosaicked base is a fully protected one. None of this is a beauty meter; all three gradients (efficiency, stealth, durability) independently point at terraced, curved, tiled, symmetric construction, and the flood itself does the presentation — twice a cycle your base becomes a lantern-lit water garden.

### Goal 8 — why you're doing this

Outer frame: the Saltmoon is falling — visibly larger each week, tides swelling with it — and the drowned Tidewright civilization left seabed engines that once tuned the tides, deliberately shut down for reasons told environmentally in ruin etchings, discoverable in any order. The moon can't be stopped, but the engines can re-tune the world into something survivable — and each server chooses its own equilibrium, because engines don't delete amplitude, they redistribute it: damping your lagoon means its seabed never fully exposes (safe but poor), while cranking a mining region's amplitude exposes deeper ore under more monstrous floods. Cascade to the moment: (1) the AMPLITUDE CLOCK — moonfall means this week's spring high tops last week's wall, so tonight's task (raise it, seal it, or float it) flows directly from the sky you can see; (2) ENGINE HUNGER — found engines demand moonsteel and resonance parts from deep ruins, which drives expeditions, which drives the automation that supports expeditions; (3) THE ALMANAC — a tide chart showing "spring low over the tin trench in 5h 12m" turns the narrative into a scheduler, so you always know why NOW; (4) MOONSPAWN ECOLOGY — predators breed in high-amplitude water, so the same falling moon explains combat, and damping an engine visibly thins the spawn — you can watch your purpose work. No quest chain: ruins, engines, and the final tuning are order-free and open-ended.

## Multiplayer (2–20, persistent)

Persistent 2-20 player servers where the shared tide clock is the social heartbeat: "spring low at 9pm, everyone on for the trench run" is the native raid night. Division of labor falls out of the phases and the systems — ebb-runners (harvest sprints, causeway building), wallwrights (flood defense, pumps, walls), tenders (weirs, pans, sluice scheduling), and divers (bell-tended deep teams, which mechanically need a surface tender and haulers). Different-timezone players inherit different tide phases of the same base, so a server naturally runs shifts. Offline, your automation IS your character: pumps, anodes, lure-beacons, and harpoon batteries hold the base through floods you're not there for — that's the honest reason automation exists — and personal goods can be sealed in cofferdams that predators won't crack. Pressure scales with population through wake: more players means more industry, more heat and turbulence, and proportionally heavier moonspawn landings, plus more coastline of wall to hold; small groups run quieter, smaller-signature bases.

## Closest existing games

- Factorio — logistics are pulsed water, not belts; power has phase
- Timberborn — shares water engineering, but real-time co-op combat survival, not colony sim
- Valheim — shares explore-to-unlock-home loop; systemic tide clock replaces biome bosses
- Don't Starve Together — shares co-op dread rhythm, adds deep persistent automation
- Subnautica — shares depth-as-menace progression; here the ocean leaves twice an hour

## Designer-admitted risks

- The flow model is the whole game and is genuinely hard in-browser at multiplayer scale: it must be a coarse, deterministic, cell-based approximation cheap enough for a fly.io node yet honest enough that sluice logistics and the laminar-aesthetics gradient feel real — if water feels fake, three pillars collapse at once.
- A shared real-time tide clock collides with real schedules: a player whose free hour always lands on flood phase, or whose timezone never sees a spring low, gets a structurally worse game; every phase needs first-class activities and the almanac needs catch-up windows, or timezone luck hardens into role inequality.
- Cycle-prep can rot into a 40-minute upkeep tax: if automation doesn't genuinely retire each tier of flood chores (bailing, hauling, watch-standing), constant pressure (goal 1) degenerates into rote maintenance — the knife-edge between rhythm and chore is the core tuning problem.
- Depth-gating can quietly re-linearize progression into copper-then-tin-then-moonsteel, defeating goal 6; each elevation band needs parallel, non-substitutable value and the artifact-recipe shuffle must be strong enough that server mid-games actually diverge.
- Offline loss tuning is brutal: a spring high plus a heavy moonspawn landing overnight can delete a solo player's smeltery; if offline threat is too real players churn, too fake and survivability automation (and the multiplayer shift fantasy) loses its point.

**Biggest weakness.** The entire design load-bears on one system: a water model that must simultaneously be cheap enough for a fly.io node syncing up to 20 real-time browser clients, deterministic, and rich enough that sluice logistics, turbine siting, wake stealth, and laminar aesthetics all read as honest. Four of the eight goals plus the unique hook route through that single sim, the pitch admits it in its own risk list, and it offers no mitigation and no fallback design. If the water turns out to be a lookup table, players discover it within a week and goals 2, 3, 4, and 7 collapse at once.

**How a real server dies (failure scenario).** Hour 30, week two, a four-player server. The crew has reverse-engineered the water model — flood is a per-tile height ramp and 'turbulence' is a penalty flag on corner pieces — so the meta base is a wiki-copied spiral of prefab curve tiles, every server converges on the same layout, and sluice 'logistics' has degenerated to 'open all gates at flood, close them at ebb' because gate timing offers one meaningful decision per 40-minute cycle. Meanwhile the weekly spring low keeps landing at 2 a.m. for the two European players, who have spent every session bailing and repairing walls while the Americans take every trench dive and engine hunt; one of them stops logging in. With that shift empty, the next overtopping spring high plus a wake-scaled reaver landing deletes the shared smeltery and power buffer overnight, and rather than re-grind a week of infrastructure that automation was supposed to protect, the server goes dark.

**The steelman fix.** Commit to proudly discrete tile-hydraulics as a visible rule language (Oxygen Not Included / Timberborn, not fluid illusion): water in legible cells, with turbulence, wake, and silt derived from a countable tile grammar players can read and master — and prototype that model under 20-client netcode before a single art asset exists. Every other pillar survives coarsening; the fatal bet that coarse water will 'feel real' is replaced by a solvable, teachable puzzle language. As a rider, let the tide period drift like real lunar tides so spring windows precess through wall-clock time and no timezone permanently owns the trench runs.

**Distinctiveness (4/5).** Closest game: Timberborn. Timberborn owns grid water engineering with periodic flood/drought crises, but it is a pauseable single-player colony sim — nobody has shipped pulsed-tide logistics as the conveyor layer of a real-time co-op survival game, so the combination differs enough to matter even though every individual part exists somewhere.

**Feasibility (2/5).** The expensive part is the deterministic whole-map flood/drain simulation synced to up to 20 browser clients on a single fly.io node — massive state churn twice per 40 minutes, plus every structure, creature, and route needing both drowned and drained states, which roughly doubles content cost exactly as the hook brags ('everything works twice'). On top of that sit real-time combat, a second underwater movement/render mode, offline-raid persistence, and viability-guaranteed procgen across wildly varying elevation histograms. Nothing here is impossible, but the honest schedule is Factorio-shaped, not small-team-browser-shaped.
