# 4. EMBERLINE

> Seed: `frost` · Goal fit **33/40** · Distinctiveness **4/5** · Feasibility **2/5** · Verdict: **promising**

**Pitch.** A real-time co-op survival-automation game on a volcanic world locked in eternal winter, where heat is simultaneously power, currency, ammunition, and life support. You tap geothermal fissures, build steam-pressure networks and meltwater flow-logistics that can literally freeze solid, and push back a physically simulated advancing icefront — while everything warm you build glows like a beacon to the things that live in the cold. Frostpunk's dread, Factorio's logistics brain, no script: the map itself is the campaign.

**The hook.** A co-op factory game where your conveyor belts are rivers of hot water the enemy can freeze, and the progress bar is the literal line of ice your machines have pushed back from your door.

**Tone.** Isometric 2D with a hand-lit look: near-monochrome blue-grey world where every warm thing is emissive amber — bases read as constellations of ember against static ice, frost shaders creep visibly across anything losing heat. Emotional register is cozy defiance: tense, whiteout expeditions punctuated by the relief of coming home to a glowing, humming machine-hearth. (Cheap to ship: tile-based world, palette + emissive/frost shaders do most of the aesthetic work, spine-style 2D creature animation.)

## How it serves the founding goals

### Goal 1 — constant pressure

The enemy is entropy, expressed three ways that never turn off. (1) HEAT LEAK: every player, machine, crop, and pipe loses heat every tick based on real exposed-surface geometry; a base is a bucket with holes, and fuel/steam income must beat the leak or sections frost over and stall (frozen smelter, burst pipe, hypothermic player). (2) THE ICEFRONT: a literal glacial edge simulated at region scale — your net heat output vs. the region's 'cold budget' decides whether it retreats or advances toward your walls; deeper map regions have bigger budgets, so equilibrium always re-forms and you can never globally win, only hold a longer line. Blizzard seasons periodically shove the front back and spike leak rates (forecastable via barometer towers, so it's planning pressure, not gotcha pressure). (3) THE PLUME: your total heat signature is Factorio-pollution-made-diegetic — a visible shimmer that scales predator aggression and awakens worse things (Rimehound packs at small plumes; heat-differential-feeding Coldwrought machines and Firn Wyrms at industrial plumes). Pressure scales with your own success, not a timer, which is why it never expires. It stays interesting rather than chore-like because the chores are the market for automation: frost-scraping, restoking, and pipe repair are all automatable, and what remains late-game is strategic pressure (where is the front, what did the thaw release, which fissure is dying) rather than bar-filling.

### Goal 2 — the automation layers

The metaphor: you are not moving items on belts, you are moving HEAT through a hostile medium, and your logistics network is alive — it can cool, clog, and freeze. Tier-0 manual tasks: hand-stoking stoves with embercoal, cranking bellows, chipping ice for water at a thaw-basin, sledge-hauling ore, scraping frost off machines, carrying warm stones in your coat to survive expeditions. Layers: (1) STEAM GRID — Fissure Taps + copper steampipes + radiator valves replace hand-stoking; heat from a tapped vent distributed to rooms and machines, with pressure loss over distance making layout a real engineering problem. (2) MELTWATER CHANNELS — heated aqueducts that carry items by flow (your 'belt' is hot water); they follow terrain slope, jam at sharp corners, and freeze if the network cools, so cold snaps are logistics attacks; replaces sledge-hauling. (3) PRESSURE MACHINERY — steam hammers (auto-smithing), bellows engines (auto-smelting), sluice sorters; replaces crafting grind. (4) STEAM LOBBERS — pneumatic launchers that fire insulated cargo pods across frozen gaps too cold for channels; replaces long-haul expeditions for goods. (5) STOKER AUTOMATONS — brass crab-bots powered by Heat Cells that defrost machines, patch pipes, and restoke remote outposts; replaces maintenance chores; gated on organs from combat (thermic bladders). (6) GOVERNORS & WHISTLE LINES — thermostats, frost sensors, and steam-whistle signal wires enabling closed-loop control: auto-divert heat to a wall under siege, auto-mothball a wing during blizzard. Freshness vs. Factorio: belts never fight back; here the logistics substrate is a fluid the antagonist (cold) can attack, every machine doubles as life support, and throughput problems are thermodynamics problems (insulate, buffer, shorten runs) rather than lane-balancing.

### Goal 2 — the sacrifice economy

Heat is one fungible budget spent on three competing accounts — staying alive (radiators, greenhouse, thaw-basin), producing (smelter, forge, channels), and investing (bore drills, new taps, automaton charging) — and the UI shows one honest number: net thermal balance. Every automation buy is paid in warmth you feel: divert steam to the steam hammer and your quarters' radiator dims tonight; charge stoker automatons and the greenhouse drops below germination temperature; extend pipes to a new vent and your plume grows, drawing predators, while every meter of pipe adds permanent leak. Territory is a sacrifice axis too: mothballing a wing (deliberately letting it freeze) reclaims budget at the cost of re-thaw labor later. WORKED EXAMPLE, MINUTE 30: your first crude Fissure Tap yields 3 steam; the smelter (2 steam) would make copper pipe for a second tap, your hut radiator (2 steam) stops nighttime health/craft-speed loss, and the thaw-basin (2 steam) melts a scavenged seal carcass for food plus a frost gland — the first blizzard lands in ~10 minutes and you can fully run exactly one. WORKED EXAMPLE, HOUR 20: a seismic event is killing your main fissure (output -5%/hour) and opened a virgin vent field 800m north inside Rimehound territory; option A, build a bore drill to deepen the dying fissure — 2 hours of diverted forge heat, greenhouse goes cold, food stores dip 40%; option B, fight north and build a steam-lobber relay chain — combat risk plus a longer plume that raises the siege tier; option C, mothball the smelting wing and shrink to equilibrium until blizzard season passes — safe, but the icefront regains 200m and reburies a ruin you'd exposed. All three are played by real groups; all three hurt.

### Goal 4 — the map changes the plan

The generator varies fissure distribution (one supervolcano caldera vs. scattered weak vents vs. linear rift), terrain slope (meltwater channels are gravity-fed), prevailing blizzard direction from the cold pole, fault lines (seismic risk zones that also birth new vents), geyser fields (periodic free heat, unreliable), and what the local ice holds (ruins, skyfall iron, frozen megafauna). Examples: dealt a scattered-weak-vent map, you go nomadic — light relay outposts, steam lobbers, low plume, abandoning each vent as it wanes; dealt one rich caldera on a fault line, you build a dense fortress with obsidian heat banks and seismic-dampened architecture, accepting quake damage as rent; dealt hard prevailing wind, you build windbreak-wedge bases open only leeward, and your defense concentrates on one face because predators also stalk downwind of your plume; dealt steep terrain, channel logistics are nearly free downhill and you site industry below the vent and quarters above it.

### Goal 5 — base ⇄ combat loop

Base-to-combat: (1) your expedition endurance is a Heat Cell charged at base boilers — an oxygen-tank mechanic where boiler capacity literally equals how deep into the cold you can raid; (2) your forge's sustained temperature ceiling (a continuous stat of your thermal engineering) determines workable weapon alloys — bronze lance to steel to skyfall-iron 'starsteel' — with no research button, just hotter fire; (3) base defense IS plumbing: pipe-fed steam-curtain walls, vent-trap kill corridors, and whistle-line-triggered lance turrets mean a well-designed steam grid is a fortress for free. Combat-to-base: (1) Steamdrinker leeches (pipe parasites) drop thermic bladders, the component for portable Heat Cells and stoker automatons; (2) a Firn Wyrm's gullet-stones line a Wyrmforge crucible — a combat-path alternative to deep boring for top-tier smelting temperatures; (3) Rimehound frost glands build cryo-condensers: food preservation, cold-traps that freeze attackers, and refrigerated logistics for goods that spoil when warm. Exploration feeds home too: the retreating icefront exposes Kindled ruins holding schematics (new machine ideas) and skyfall iron.

### Goals 3 + 6 — many paths, no milestones

The tech tree is replaced by a thermometer and a pressure gauge: capabilities are continuous physical thresholds (smelting alloys, glass, ceramics, sterilization, forging) reached by ANY engineering route — cluster many small taps, bore one deep shaft, burn hoarded rare fuels, or line a crucible with wyrm gullet-stones — so 'progression' is your best sustained temperature and pressure, which ramps smoothly and can even regress. Discrete novelty comes from Kindled schematics found in thaw-exposed ruins in whatever order your icefront happens to uncover them, and from Great Vents (mega-fissures) that can be rekindled in any order, each a different multi-system siege rather than a quest step. Three discovered archetypes: CALDERA FORTRESS — tall, centralized, maximum insulation and heat banking, rides out sieges, vulnerable to quakes and fissure decline; VEIN RIDERS — nomadic low-plume relay play that hops dying vents, heavy on automatons and lobbers, nearly invisible to apex threats, thin margins; THE LURE — deliberately radiating bait-heat to ranch predators through vent-trap corridors, running an organ economy (bladders, glands, scales) that buys what industry otherwise would; plus an ICEBOUND variant that embraces cold — igloo-physics ice-block construction (excellent free insulation) and cryo-industry with a minimal thermal core.

### Goal 7 — beauty pays rent

Beauty is thermal truth, enforced by three native rules. (1) Heat loss is computed on real geometry, so compact courtyard forms, symmetric radiator placement, and windbreak curves are OPTIMAL — the mandala base around a central fissure is the efficient base. (2) Meltwater channels suffer turbulence-freezing at sharp corners and junctions, so graceful sweeping curves outperform grid zigzags — your logistics literally flows better when it looks like calligraphy. (3) EVENWARM: enclosed rooms get a regen/craft-speed bonus derived from low temperature VARIANCE across their tiles, not high average — achieving even warmth demands thoughtful radiator spacing, door placement, and proportioned rooms, which is indistinguishable from interior design. The renderer closes the loop: warm surfaces glow amber, inefficiency blooms as ragged frost, so a screenshot of your base IS its thermal diagnostic — a well-engineered base photographs like stained glass, a botched one looks visibly mangy. No beauty meter exists; the physics just happens to have taste.

### Goal 8 — why you're doing this

Frame: you are heirs of the Kindled, whose planetary forge-network once kept this volcanic world warm; when it failed, the ice took everything, and your outer goal is to REKINDLE the world — push the frost back, region by region. Four mechanisms cascade from that to the current second: (1) the icefront is the progress bar — a physical line on the shared map whose position is the sum of every boiler stoked and every watt leaked, so even hand-feeding a stove is visibly 'fighting the ice'; (2) the thaw is the content faucet — retreating ice exposes ruins, schematics, skyfall iron, and new biomes, so exploration rewards flow FROM the survival-industrial effort rather than beside it; (3) Great Vents are the open-order landmarks — rekindling each dormant mega-fissure permanently shifts a region's cold budget and each demands a different build (one needs a pressure network, one a fighting retreat through Coldwrought, one a logistics marathon), pickable in any order or ignored; (4) the ice remembers — thawing releases what it held (the Pale Herd, dormant Coldwrought, buried plagues), so the narrative pushes back through the same systems, and 'why am I doing this' always answers itself: because the line is right there, and it moved last night.

## Multiplayer (2–20, persistent)

Persistent 2-20 player servers on a shared thermal grid. Division of labor falls out of the systems, not classes: someone gravitates to the steam grid and governors (engineer), someone to Heat-Cell-funded deep expeditions (ranger), someone to insulation geometry and Evenwarm interiors (architect), someone to greenhouse and thaw-basin food chains (steward) — all fungible, since every role spends the same heat budget, which also makes group priority arguments the good kind of conflict. Offline handling: when the server empties, simulation drops to a dormancy tick (roughly 10x slow, blizzard cycle paused) while heat banks drain slowly — so building obsidian heat-bank buffer capacity is literally designing for your own absence, and returning players perform a short, automatable 'relighting' ritual scaled to how long the base slept. Pressure scales endogenously with population: more players means more machines, a bigger plume, a faster-retreating icefront, and proportionally larger sieges and cold budgets — the game gets bigger, not merely easier, with friends.

## Closest existing games

- Factorio — pollution/aggro loop, but logistics medium is freezable heat, not belts
- Frostpunk — heat-as-life pressure, without scripted scenarios or city-abstraction
- Oxygen Not Included — real thermodynamic simulation, but real-time co-op and combat-forward
- Valheim — expedition-and-homecoming loop with free boss order, plus deep automation
- Don't Starve Together — persistent co-op survival tone, with an actual industrial endgame

## Designer-admitted risks

- Simulation cost in browser: per-tile heat diffusion plus fluid-flow channels for 20 players' megabase will not run client-side or naively server-side; it must be abstracted to a network-graph model (nodes and lumped capacitances), and that abstraction risks killing the 'real physics' feel that the aesthetics mechanic and continuous progression both depend on.
- Death-spiral tuning: heat economies snowball downward (freeze reduces production which accelerates freeze); Frostpunk managed this with scripted arcs, and an unscripted persistent server can end up unrecoverable-but-not-dead — the worst state for a multiplayer world. Needs designed brakes (mothballing, dormancy, icefront equilibrium) that don't read as rubber-banding.
- Plume-scaling can punish the core fantasy: if heat-attracts-threat is tuned wrong, the dominant strategy becomes staying small and dark, which is anti-Factorio; the reward curve for going tall must provably beat turtling, and The Lure archetype must not trivialize the organ economy.
- Physics-derived aesthetics may converge on one optimal shape — if every efficient base is the same radial mandala, goal 3 (many solutions) and goal 7 quietly fight each other; the generator's terrain, wind, and vent asymmetries have to be strong enough to force varied optima, and that is a tuning bet, not a guarantee.
- Offline dormancy is exploitable and tonally risky: pausing blizzards when nobody is on invites log-off-to-dodge play and undermines 'constant pressure'; the alternative (world keeps hurting you while you sleep) kills retention on small servers. The heat-bank-as-absence-insurance design is a hypothesis, not a proven solution.

**Biggest weakness.** The pitch's most distinctive claims — heat leak on real exposed-surface geometry, Evenwarm per-tile variance, frost-bloom-as-thermal-diagnostic, corner-turbulence freezing — all require exactly the simulation fidelity that its own risk #1 concedes cannot run for a 20-player browser megabase. Abstract to the lumped node-graph the risk proposes and goals 6 and 7 lose their mechanical substance; keep the fidelity and the game doesn't ship. The design's entire identity rests on a simulation budget the platform doesn't have, and the pitch flags the contradiction without resolving it.

**How a real server dies (failure scenario).** Hour 25 of a persistent 8-friend server, Tuesday night: only two regulars log in. The weekend crew built an industrial plume, so siege tier and heat demand are scaled to eight players' output. A forecast cold snap plus a Coldwrought raid freeze a meltwater artery; frozen channels halt embercoal to the boilers; net balance goes negative and the freeze-reduces-production-accelerates-freeze spiral begins. Two players cannot patch pipes, restoke, and repel the siege at once, so they do the mechanically correct thing: log off. The server empties, dormancy kicks in, blizzards pause, and heat banks preserve the base — the world is objectively safer unplayed than played. Within a week the group's meta is 'never log in alone, the base does better without you'; midweek sessions stop, the persistent world degrades into a weekend lobby, and the pitch's fifth risk has become the retention curve.

**The steelman fix.** Commit to the lumped thermal-graph model from day one — rooms as heat capacitors, walls/doors/pipes as conductances — and re-derive Evenwarm, leak, and channel-freezing natively at room granularity instead of promising tile-level physics you'll have to fake. This makes the browser/server simulation affordable, makes netcode tractable, and turns the anti-convergence problem into tunable graph parameters rather than emergent-physics hope; sell 'real geometry' honestly as room topology, which is what players actually design anyway.

**Distinctiveness (4/5).** Closest game: Oxygen Not Included. ONI already sells thermodynamics-as-substrate survival-automation, but no shipped title combines it with real-time co-op, a territorial icefront war, and combat-forward expeditions — every component exists somewhere, yet the combination is genuinely unoccupied space.

**Feasibility (2/5).** The cost center is the server-authoritative real-time thermal-plus-fluid simulation for persistent 20-player worlds: per-tile diffusion is out of the question on a fly.io-class box, so a graph abstraction and aggressive interest-management netcode must be engineered before any content exists — and that abstraction IS the game, not a supporting system. The palette-and-shader art savings are real but don't offset it, and procedural terrain that must produce hydrology-valid slopes for gravity-fed channels adds a second hard-to-fake system.
