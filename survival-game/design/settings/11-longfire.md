# 11. LONGFIRE

> Round 2 (player-seeded) · Seed: `early human colony` · Goal fit **28/40** · Distinctiveness **4/5** · Feasibility **2/5** · Verdict: **promising**

**Pitch.** A real-time co-op survival game about the first human settlement in an untouched land, where the factory you build is made of people. You start as one named figure knapping flint with your own hands; you end leading a living colony whose weirs, kilns, herds, and rites run as standing orders held together by mortal memory — because every practice your civilization owns lives in someone's teachable, ageing, forgettable head, and the constant enemy is not just hunger but entropy: of food, of goodwill, of knowledge itself.

**The hook.** The tech tree is the population — every technology lives in a mortal head until you build the culture that can outlive one — so civilization is not something you unlock, it's something you keep lit.

**Tone.** A sun-washed river valley in painted-pottery colors — ochre, charcoal, madder, bone-white — rendered as chunky 2D isometric sprites over textures that read like woven cloth and cave-wall paint, with firelight doing the atmospheric heavy lifting (cheap to draw, mechanically load-bearing). Emotionally it is campfire-epic: warm, elegiac, occasionally grim — the feeling of living inside a saga that is being sung about you while you can still change how it ends.

## How it serves the founding goals

### Goal 1 — constant pressure

Four pressures, all keyed to footprint and presence, none solvable. (1) THE WHEEL: the seasonal cycle advances on aggregate player presence — diegetically, the saga only moves when it is being told — so winters arrive in proportion to hours actually played, and preparation for the next lean season is a permanent line item: stores are rates on a visible ledger, and a colony of 60 eats like a fire. (2) FORGETTING: people age and die on the same presence-scaled clock, and every practice — pressure-flaking, kiln-firing, flood-terracing — decays without teaching, use, or memory works. This pressure *grows with capability*: the more your civilization knows, the more it can forget, so entropy of memory scales exactly as fast as your progress and never goes away. (3) ENVY AND APPETITE: footprint attracts. Cleared fields, visible smoke, and full granaries raise rival bands' raid-omen meters (scaled to hoard and territory, never to calendar); hunted herds thin and migrate away, pushing expansion; felled groves and dammed channels raise the displeasure of the land's spirits, each with its own appetite meter. Staying small genuinely buys quiet. (4) DRIFT: standing orders lose fidelity across distance, time, and headcount — the whisper problem — surfacing as stalled bands, disputes, and rumor, scaling with colony size. The pressures stay interesting because they trade against each other: clearing more fields (hunger) feeds envy; a season of teaching (forgetting) takes hands from the harvest; appeasing the River-Grandmother (appetite) burns the stores that were your winter answer; every fix to drift costs childhood-years of schooling. Per Rule 3, the Wheel guarantees the lull — deep winter and high summer are structurally quiet seasons where raids and floods don't stack, and the hearth is for planning, teaching, and building.

### Goal 2 — the automation layers

The metaphor: colonists are the machines, and the verbs of automation are social — teach, delegate, ritualize, record — not mechanical. Tier-0 manual life: you personally forage, knap flint, haul water skins, scrape hides, grind einkorn on a saddle-quern, stack the first stone, carry the firewood, stand the night watch. The layers below are a *set*, not a sequence — any can be reached first, several can be skipped entirely, and no run needs all of them. WORK-BANDS: assign hands to a repeating task at a place — gather-band on the berry slopes, weir-band at the river — running as pure throughput arithmetic (hands × practice skill × tool quality = rate; Rule 14 is the design language, and the Speaker literally reports in rates). Alleviates doing everything with your own two hands; introduces drift and disputes that escalate to you. Crucially, bands feed *themselves*: a hearth-band cooks and distributes as its own standing order, so food is an internal rate, never a spoon-feeding chore — the player's cost is leadership, not meal service (this is the anti-upkeep-treadmill commitment: every layer must net positive on attention or it doesn't ship). CRAFT PRACTICES: techniques plus fixed works — the kiln, the loom, the granary, the weir, the smelting pit — that multiply band throughput and retire specific resented chores (the quern, the hide-scraper, the water run). New demands: fuel and feedstock lines, maintenance, and raid bait — a granary is wealth with a smoke plume. FOREMEN AND SPEAKERS: named colonists holding delegated authority who resolve small escalations locally and keep orders true near them. Alleviates escalation spam; introduces succession, pride, and faction — a foreman is a machine that can want things. MEMORY WORKS: song-lines, tally-clay, painted workshop walls, and eventually true script — orders stop drifting, practices stop dying with their last practitioner. Alleviates re-teaching and re-ordering; introduces schooling, priced in childhood-years, a currency you will fight over. PACTS: bound spirits doing world-scale labor — the River-Grandmother holds her banks, the Antler King keeps the forest paths open, the Kiln-Wight self-stokes your firings. Alleviates whole categories of work; introduces appetites and taboos that constrain your *other* systems (see below). DAUGHTER-HEARTHS: outposts run by foremen plus memory tech at distance. Alleviates long hauls and local exhaustion; introduces caravan lines to defend and divergence — outposts develop their own ways, and can schism. Why this is fresh: against Factorio, the machines have names, age, argue, and die, and logistics means keeping instructions true through mortal minds rather than optimizing belt geometry. Against RimWorld and Dwarf Fortress, you never puppet individuals — you lead aggregates that run as legible arithmetic, individuals surface only as events (a death, a dispute, a prodigy), and there is an embodied *you* inside the system, eating from the same stores.

### Goal 2 — the sacrifice economy

One economy: hands-hours, food stores, materials (timber, hide, sinew, clay, salt, copper), childhood-years, renown, and offerings all draw from the pools survival needs. There is no research currency; "research" is a master's labor not spent producing. Worked minute-30 decision (band of nine): the herd passes the ford in two days. Sef, your only knapper, can finish six spearheads in that window — the hunt lands roughly 400 pounds of meat, twenty days of food — or she can spend those two days teaching pressure-flaking to Odu, producing nothing. Teach, and you miss the herd: stores drop to four days, the band forages thin for a week, and you personally go hungry on watch — but the craft now lives in two heads, and Sef coughed all winter. Worked hour-20 decision (colony of ~40): the spring flood took the weir, your largest food rate. (a) Rebuild it with the new post-and-lattice practice: 60 worked timbers plus a band of eight for six days, pulled off the barley harvest — winter stores land ~15% short, rationing by autumn. (b) Feed the River-Grandmother instead: ten sealed jars of grain and the season's first catch, and she holds the channel for a full year — but she claims the drowned meadow, your best expansion land, as taboo ground. (c) Send for the eastern band's flood-terracing practice: a bride-price of copper and your only iron blade, plus your own Named gone three days escorting — during a raid-omen window, with the colony's exception-handler absent. Tech, mysticism, and diplomacy collide in one decision, and every option mortgages a different future for at least a full threat cycle (Rule 4).

### Goal 4 — the map changes the plan

The generator varies river topology, biomes, deposits (clay, flint, salt springs, copper, tin, amber), herd behavior (migratory versus resident), the *spirit roster* — which Unseen inhabit this land, with what appetites and taboos, meaning the mysticism "tree" itself is map-dealt — and the neighboring bands, each seeded with a distinct practice set, because knowledge-as-loot lives in neighbors, ruins, and dreams. Every seed is denied at least one comfort. Examples: (1) No clay within two days' walk — so no pottery, so no sealed granary jars; we pivoted to smoke-drying racks and the salt spring, and salt surplus made us the region's trade hub: the map forced a culture-and-diplomacy build. (2) The herds rolled migratory — half the colony became a moving camp that follows them, and memory works jumped the queue because keeping standing orders true across *two sites* is impossible on song alone: the map forced literacy before agriculture. (3) This seed's Kiln-Wight is generous but iron-hating — self-stoked firings from week one, but the first iron bloom in that district sours every kiln in it: the map made mysticism and metallurgy mutually exclusive per district, and we built our smithy a valley away, with a caravan line we now have to guard.

### Goal 5 — base ⇄ combat loop

Base to field: smelted copper and sinew-backed bows arm hunt-bands the base fed and trained; rites priced in offerings buff a named hunt or defense (war-paint from ochre, courage from the ancestor fire); palisades, dog packs, and watchfires are base production; and territory your singers have song-mapped gives bands local-knowledge bonuses — you ambush better on ground your culture holds. Field to base: a great-beast carcass is an industry shipment (sinew for bows and looms, bone for tools, hide for armor and glue); a defeated rival band yields *practices* — captives or guests, your call, with cultural consequences either way — plus their salt spring as territory; a subdued spirit-wrath drops a binding token that opens pact options no rite could reach; ruins hold practice fragments only expeditions recover. Per Rule 11, every base upgrade path requires at least one field-only input — ore, beast parts, spirit tokens, foreign teachers — and every field capability traces back to hearth production. The fighter and the weaver are each other's suppliers by construction.

### Goals 3 + 6 — many paths, no milestones

There is no tech tree UI and there are no eras. Capability = the set of practices currently alive in your people's heads, plus the works standing, plus the pacts held — all continuously priced in materials, hands, teachers, and risk, attemptable at any time in any order. The seed's "effective tiers" are *emergent phase changes in continuous quantities*, not gates: when food-per-hand crosses roughly 1.2, hands come free and specialists appear; when memory fidelity exceeds colony size, daughter-hearths become stable; when renown crosses a neighbor's envy, raids turn to embassies. Different servers cross different thresholds in different orders — steppe seeds get tally-stones before agriculture; river seeds get granaries before script — and order itself is strategy. The speedrun test passes: you can have bronze in hour three by walking your Named to the mountain band and trading half your stores and most of your renown for a smith's teaching — painful, legal, occasionally brilliant. Archetypes: (1) THE GRANARY KINGS — agriculture, memory works, walls: big, literate, sedentary; maximum envy, answered with awe and stores. (2) THE SONG ROAD — few crops, many pacts, much trade: a small, beautiful hearth on a crossroads, civilization as network; wealth without footprint, fragile to a neighbor's collapse. (3) THE HERDWALKERS — mobile, herd-following, ancestor-heavy: knowledge carried in song rather than walls, low envy, raid-capable; forfeits heavy industry by choice. (4) THE COVENANT — deep mysticism: half the colony's labor done by bound spirits, tech hemmed by taboo, terrifying to rivals; permanently one broken appetite from cascade. Each treats a different pressure as primary, and the Evening Saga (below) always lists more worthwhile work than any headcount can clear (Rule 6) — when automation catches up, the known-line, the neighbors, and the Unseen expand the frontier.

### Goal 7 — beauty pays rent

THE HEARTH-GROUND. The simulation reads emergent properties of the working settlement — firelight sightlines, open gathering space, symmetry of the circle, variety of crafts visible from the hearth, scale, living trees and water inside the bounds — and three core systems pay for them. (1) Wanderers: recruitment, the manpower economy's main external input, is driven by renown, and travelers judge you by your hearth — skilled strangers walk into beautiful settlements and past windowless boxes. (2) Spirits: each seed's Unseen have layout tastes (the River-Grandmother wants open water sightlines; the Antler King wants living trees among the houses), and a spirit *in residence* makes every pact cheaper — your shrine's efficacy is computed from the place, not from a decoration checklist. (3) Memory: practices taught in painted, legible, well-lit workshops drift and fade slower — murals are literal storage, so beauty directly fights the game's signature pressure. All yield arrives as world logic through mechanics that already run everything else — people and spirits come to beautiful places; knowledge sticks to decorated walls — never as a beauty meter. The lean box stays viable (low footprint means low envy; you can recruit by births and record on tally-clay) but it strictly forfeits wanderers, resident spirits, and mural memory, so it never dominates; and because the scored properties are composable, unlimited layouts score high — there is no template to copy.

### Goal 8 — why you're doing this

The frame: your band fled the Long Winter that ate the old country, carrying two things — the Ember, the last coal of your people's hearth, and the Roll of Names. The outer goal is continuity: make this land home so the fire never dies and no name is forgotten. The cascade, with no quest log: (1) THE ROLL OF NAMES — a live, server-shared tally of the living, the remembered dead, and the forgotten; every system traces to it, and the UI shows the stakes of the thing in your hand ("this weir feeds eleven names"; "if Sef dies untaught, her craft dies with her"). (2) THE EVENING SAGA — each in-game evening, the Speaker recites what the simulation currently sees: the herds thin in the east, the River-Grandmother unfed since the flood, Maro's kiln-craft without an apprentice, the western band's envy rising. It is a sim-generated, server-shared objective surface (Rule 13) that players triage in their own order; ignoring an item has consequences, never fail-states. (3) THE WHEEL — the seasonal forecast makes preparation legible: everyone can see winter's distance against the stores rate. (4) THE SONG ITSELF — deeds are sung, renown is a currency, and even your own character's continuity is priced inside the memory economy (below). Walk any why-chain and it lands on the Ember: why smelt copper? — to arm the hunt — to fill the winter jars — so the names on the Roll stay in the living column.

## Multiplayer (2–20, persistent)

Every player is one of the NAMED — saga-figures of a single shared band; the colony is common property like a Factorio factory, with every order and edit attributed in the saga log, and no verb that dismantles faster than a session rebuilds (Rule 15 — abandonment is gradual, arson isn't shipped). Division of labor emerges from order-authorship domains and frontier verbs — huntmaster, diplomat, rite-keeper, works-planner — voluntary and fluid, never class-gated, and no task requires simultaneous button-pressing. The character stays load-bearing at hour 30 because only a Named can: push the known-line (standing orders bind only on song-mapped ground, so expansion is protagonist scouting plus song-mapping); make first contact and swear pacts (spirits and chiefs deal with persons, not policies); *first-acquire* a practice from a world source — a ruin, a dream, a rival master — since only embodied knowledge can seed the colony's; lead great hunts as a force multiplier; and handle every exception no standing order covers. And the Named stays pressured: you eat from the same stores, your absence on expedition is priced (the exception-handler is gone during the omen window), and defeat is bounded per Rule 1 — your Named falls, is carried home, and within minutes you take up a new name from the band's youths, inheriting skills in proportion to how well your deeds were sung. Your own progression is subject to the memory system. Offline, logging off is "stepping out of the song": bands run standing orders as fast-forwarded rate integrals, stores fill and draw down, drift and omen meters rise visibly — but nothing strikes while no Named is present, and the Wheel advances on aggregate presence so a quiet week never faces winter alone. The day-30 join: a wanderer drawn by the Hearth-Ground's renown walks in — onboarding is literally fed by the beauty mechanic — and frontier verbs (scouting, caravan guard, holding a wall, courting the western band, apprenticing under Sef) need zero catch-up track; one Evening Saga recital hands the newcomer the server's live to-do. Pressure scaling: expedition and rite risk key to the players present; envy, appetite, and drift key to footprint regardless of headcount — a large colony with two players online is a tenser, quieter game than the same colony with twelve, never the same raid wave.

## Closest existing games

- RimWorld — you lead rate-based bands, embodied, not puppeting pawns
- Songs of Syx — population-as-rates, but embodied, co-op, and spirit-priced
- Valheim — embodied co-op survival, plus an actual colony engine
- Dwarf Fortress — knowledge dies with mortals, but legible, multiplayer, browser-cheap
- Banished — a logistics colony you personally walk in and bleed for

## Designer-admitted risks

- Social automation legibility: bands that stall, orders that drift, and foremen who sour must constantly explain themselves or the freshest system reads as pathfinding bugs — every stalled rate needs a one-click "why" inspector, and that UX is the real cost of the pitch.
- The aggregation boundary: bands-as-arithmetic with individuals surfacing as events is the entire feasibility bet, and choosing *when* a colonist becomes a named character (death, dispute, prodigy, teaching) is unsolved tuning with narrative stakes on both sides of the line.
- Forgetting-as-pressure flirts with Rule 1: a fading practice can read as erased progress. The floor — practices fade to "half-remembered," relearnable at steep discount, never to zero — must be generous, or losing the last potter feels like a wipe instead of a tax.
- The spirit roster needs real systemic teeth per seed — appetites and taboos that genuinely constrain tech, layout, and expansion — which is a generator-and-content investment comparable to Husklight's inspection grammar, and a flat roster collapses mysticism into a buff menu.
- Twenty protagonists authoring standing orders on one colony invites order wars among friends; attribution and gradual-abandonment bound the damage, but genuine governance friction (order precedence, domain ownership) is unmodeled and may demand tooling we haven't scoped.
- Presence-scaled world time is the load-bearing anti-"logging-off-is-optimal" patch, and it has a known seam: highly asymmetric play schedules mean one heavy player drags the Wheel for everyone — the weighting curve will need ugly invisible tuning.

**Biggest weakness.** The hook *is* cross-cutting lesson 2: the foundational move — every machine is a mortal head that ages, forgets, disputes, and dies — rebuilds the upkeep treadmill that killed or wounded four round-1 settings, then points the presence-scaled clock at it, so automation degrades fastest precisely while owned and your teammates' play hours become the force ageing your infrastructure. The designer admits forgetting-vs-Rule-1 but never notices forgetting-vs-Rule-5: either memory works fully stop decay (a checkbox, and the flagship pressure dies) or they don't (re-teaching is a permanent chore treadmill), and the dossier refuses to pick. "We commit to a floor" is an incantation; the mechanism that makes forgetting tension instead of maintenance is not in the pitch.

**How a real server dies (failure scenario).** Six-player server, week two. Marcus is retired and plays thirty hours a week; the other five play weekends. His solo hours drag the Wheel through the autumn the group provisioned against, and two elder masters age-die during his Tuesday sessions, dropping smelting and flood-terracing to half-remembered; his (fully attributed, perfectly legal) standing-order rewrite diverts three bands to a renown monument, and because nothing strikes offline, the food ledger just quietly erodes. Saturday, five players log in to deep winter, red drift meters, and an Evening Saga listing fourteen escalations — they spend the entire session re-teaching practices and untangling orders, zero frontier verbs, no lull, the Rule 6 triage-ache replaced by janitorial cleanup. The group chat finds the rational fix the design left open: ask Marcus to stop logging in, because his presence ages Grandmother and burns everyone's summer. He complies; the colony shrinks; smaller footprint quiets envy, lower capability slows forgetting, the floors guarantee the fire technically never dies — and the server settles into a painless stasis nobody returns to. It is the first survival game where the scarce resource you ration is a friend's permission to play.

**The steelman fix.** Re-key the Wheel and mortal ageing from aggregate player presence to colony throughput — labor executed and footprint held — so world time runs as fast as the civilization burns, not as long as anyone is logged in. This preserves "the saga only moves when told," deletes the hour-rationing social poison and the heavy-player seam in one stroke, aligns the entire clock stack with Rule 2's footprint principle, and turns pace itself into a collective strategic dial: run lean to stretch a summer, run hot and accept that winter — and the graveyard — come sooner.

**Distinctiveness (4/5).** Closest game: RimWorld. A tech tree stored in mortal, teachable heads on a persistent co-op server is a genuine invention no shipped game has — but it buys that distinctiveness by drifting out of the automation genre the brief names: if the factory is people, there is no throughput geometry to optimize, and the Factorio-shaped player the founding goals court may find a management sim where the belts have opinions.

**Feasibility (2/5).** The most expensive line item is legible social automation: a one-click "why" inspector over drift, disputes, succession, and faction (the designer's own risk 1) is a UX research project, not a feature, and it sits atop nightly sim-to-prose Saga generation, a hand-authored spirit roster needing systemic teeth per seed, and an unsolved aggregation boundary between colonists and Named. The advertised offline "rate integrals" are false advertising — deaths, schisms, disputes, and successions are discrete social events, not rates, so the Rule 14 fast-forward this pitch leans on hardest is exactly what its simulation cannot cheaply provide.
