# 7. SWITCHGRASS (working title — double meaning: prairie grass reclaiming the world / the switchgear you fight to restore)

> Seed: `overgrowth` · Goal fit **33/40** · Distinctiveness **4/5** · Feasibility **2/5** · Verdict: **promising**

**Pitch.** Fifty years after the flood, the grid is still out there — drowned substations, kudzu-strangled rail, greenhouses running on dead timers — and something on the shortwave is counting down to a flyover. SWITCHGRASS is a co-op survival-automation game where you don't build a factory, you resuscitate one wire by wire, while the wilderness eats everything you restore and the old world's security system wakes up angry every time you turn something on. Every watt you route is a choice between staying alive tonight and never having to do this chore again.

**The hook.** You don't build the factory — you resuscitate a drowned civilization's grid wire by wire, while the wilderness eats what you restore and the grid's own immune system hunts you for daring to turn it on.

**Tone.** Painterly isometric 2D — hand-painted tiles in the Eastward/Ghibli-decay register: warm lantern-and-sodium-light pockets against vast teal-and-rust drowned cityscapes, with animated water tiles, weather as translucent overlays, and a shared night-map view where every restored district is literally a lit pixel. Emotional register: melancholy hope — tending a campfire in a cathedral of ruins — punctuated by the dread hum of a substation booting up.

## How it serves the founding goals

### Goal 1 — constant pressure

Three interlocking pressures, each scaling with a different thing so no single mitigation kills the game. (1) THE GREENWAVE — accelerated rewilding: vines, root-heave, and silt physically reclaim any unmaintained tile. Roads crack, cable runs get strangled, solar panels get shaded, rails get buried. Crucially the entropy tax is proportional to YOUR restored footprint, so it can never be beaten — the more grid you wake, the longer your frontier of maintenance. It stays interesting because counterplay is strategic, not janitorial: route lines along defensible viaducts, cultivate hedge ecology that redirects growth (see aesthetics), reflash vine-cutter crawlers, or deliberately mothball districts you can't hold. (2) WEATHER AND WATER — a real hydrological sim at district granularity: storm fronts arrive on a forecast the radio gives you 1-2 in-game days ahead, surge raises the flood line, cold snaps burst unlagged pipes and drain batteries. Weather scales with seasons and with map climate band, and it never stops because the forecast keeps generating; it stays interesting because storms are also OPPORTUNITY (wind turbines overproduce, AEGIS optics degrade in rain — storm raids are a thing, flood lets barges reach dry-season-inaccessible ruins). (3) AEGIS, the old world's security network — dormant turrets ("Wardens"), hunter-killer drones ("Ospreys"), and patrol quadrupeds ("Shepherds") that wake in proportion to GRID LOAD in their sector. Powering a district powers its immune system. AEGIS response scales with your ambition, not a timer, so late-game industrial players face proportionally more; players who stay dark face less but pay full manual-labor cost. Plus the megafauna layer: herds trample lines on migration routes, an apex predator claims territory, rodents gnaw ground-laid cable. Baseline hunger/warmth exists but is deliberately cheap to solve manually — the real constant pressure is entropy, weather, and the grid's double edge.

### Goal 2 — the automation layers

The metaphor is RESUSCITATION, not construction: the map is pre-seeded with real, ruined infrastructure with genuine topology, and automating means splicing it back to life, defending it, and studying it. Tier-0 manual tasks: foraging and hand-fishing, hauling water and salvage on your back, hand-crank battery charging, machete-clearing vines off your lines, sandbagging before storms, splicing cable by hand, standing night watch. The layers: (L1) RECLAIMED POWER — salvaged panels, a ridge wind turbine, micro-hydro at a breached dam; kills hand-cranking, enables powered tools. (L2) WATERWORKS — restored pumping stations and sluice gates; automates water purification AND flood control (pump your district out during surge instead of sandbagging all night). (L3) GRANGE LINES — reboot automated greenhouses, vertical farms, and grain elevators still running dead schedules; kills farming/foraging labor. (L4) RAIL & DRAFT — restore rail spurs with a salvaged shunter locomotive, or the parallel living path: tame draft aurochs and pack elk as logistics; kills hauling. (L5) REFLASHED MACHINES — capture feral Shepherds and courier drones, pull their cores, reflash them: vine-cutter crawlers automate the entropy tax itself, sentry dogs automate night watch, couriers automate small freight. (L6) THE EXCHANGE — a restored fabricator line that turns bulk salvage into precision parts (actuator cores, insulation, splice kits), closing the loop so you stop strip-mining ruins. Why it's fresh vs Factorio belts: the layout is FOUND, not drawn — your creativity goes into topological repair, splicing around dead segments, and load-balancing a grid you only partially control; automation has an immune response (AEGIS + Greenwave) so every machine is contested territory, not settled ground; and there is a full living-systems alternative (animals, ecology, biofuel) running parallel to the mechanical stack.

### Goal 2 — the sacrifice economy

Four currencies the survival loop also needs. WATTS: early power is desperately scarce and everything competes for it — heater vs. tool charger vs. the inrush current needed to jump-start a substation. DUAL-USE MATERIALS: copper wire is snares, fishing rigs, and heater coils OR grid splices; machine cores run your stun-lance OR a pump; cooking oil is calories OR biodiesel. TIME-UNDER-PRESSURE: every automation project is hours not spent stockpiling food, patching the roof before the front arrives, or clearing the vines that are strangling yesterday's project. SAFETY/TERRITORY: energizing a sector wakes its AEGIS nodes; broadcasting for help is audible to Ospreys; fencing a greenhouse displaces a herd onto someone else's line. MINUTE-30 WORKED EXAMPLE: you have one salvaged 12V truck battery and night is coming with a cold snap. It can run the space heater until dawn (safe sleep, full stamina tomorrow) OR give you ten minutes of arc welder to splice the cable from camp to the ridge wind turbine — if the storm's wind holds you wake to charged everything, but the splice needs insulation tape you'd have to grab from the flooded pharmacy in the dark, past the heron marsh where the grey bear dens. Freeze tonight to never freeze again, or stay warm and stay manual. HOUR-20 WORKED EXAMPLE: the crew has the Marsh District substation ready to energize. Energizing brings a greenhouse complex and the eastern rail spur online (huge food + logistics), but the grid map shows the same bus feeds a dormant AEGIS armory with a Warden turret cluster (90-second boot per node), and the fences the greenhouse needs will displace the aurochs herd — whose likely new migration path crosses your western wire run. Live options: energize and rush-raid the armory inside the boot window; cold-raid it first to pull the Wardens' fuses (stealth, at night, no power assist); spend 40 copper splices you were saving for the water pump to cut around the armory at pylon 7; or don't energize at all and eat the ongoing labor tax of manual farming. It hurts because every option spends something the survival loop is screaming for.

### Goal 4 — the map changes the plan

Procgen varies five load-bearing axes: hydrology (flood topology, tidal vs. riverine, watertable), WHICH infrastructure survived (every map gets a partial grid — maybe the dam lives but the rail is drowned, maybe an elevated tram viaduct survived but every substation is silted), megafauna species mix and migration routes, AEGIS density and network topology, and climate band. Concrete reactions: (1) 'The power plant is drowned but an elevated tram viaduct survived → I skipped conventional grid restoration entirely, ran a battery-tram economy charged at micro-hydro, and strung my whole base along the viaduct above the flood line.' (2) 'The elephant migration route crosses the only intact substation corridor and they smash fences → I ran my lines underground through the metro tunnels instead, which meant fighting tunnel AEGIS checkpoints in the dark, which pushed my whole combat tech EMP-and-stealth early.' (3) 'High-latitude map: brutal winters, but hard freeze makes the marsh walkable → raid season is winter (cross the ice to the island armory while Osprey rotors ice up), build season is summer, and food strategy flipped from greenhouses to autumn fish-smoking stockpiles.' Because the tech 'tree' is scavenged from map locations (see nonlinearity), the map doesn't just change tactics — it changes which capabilities you can even pursue first.

### Goal 5 — base ⇄ combat loop

Base→combat: (1) a powered workshop keeps stun-lances and EMP satchels charged — and a restored substation can OVERCHARGE them, the only reliable way to drop a Warden's shields, so raiding capacity literally scales with grid capacity; (2) greenhouse output feeds a pharmacology bench — antivenom, cold-resist stims, and the sedative darts used to relocate rather than kill megafauna; (3) a restored rail spur lets you build a flatcar rig (searchlight + harpoon winch + battery bank) turning a static base into a mobile assault/rescue platform along the line. Combat/exploration→base: (1) a defeated Warden drops intact actuator cores — the gating part for automating sluice gates and fabricator arms, so flood control is literally built from your enemies' corpses; (2) pulling the black box from a downed Osprey yields its patrol schedule for the whole sector plus the reflash schematic for that chassis — every boss is a future employee; (3) raiding the flooded data center recovers grid maps that reveal buried/hidden infrastructure under the canopy (new automation opportunities you literally could not see); (4) sedating and relocating the grey bear shifts herd behavior, opening a whole district as safe pasture and greenhouse land. The loop is tight because AEGIS guards exactly the things automation needs, and automation produces exactly the tools AEGIS demands.

### Goals 3 + 6 — many paths, no milestones

There is no research screen. Capability comes from three overlapping sources, all map- and choice-dependent: (1) SCHEMATICS live in places — factory offices, libraries, black boxes, faction gifts — and which places exist varies per map; (2) WORKING REFERENCE — any machine you've restored and kept running long enough to study, you can then build copies of; apprenticeship-by-restoration means your 'tree' is literally the infrastructure you chose to wake, in the order you woke it; (3) THE DIAL — radio factions teach recipes in exchange for goods and deeds, and you cannot satisfy all of them. Progression is therefore a continuous gradient of capacity (watts stable, freight per day, sectors held, frequencies friendly) rather than milestone gates — there's no 'now you have researched X' moment, only 'the greenhouse has run a full week, we understand it now.' Three genuinely different viable archetypes: GRIDRUNNERS — wake the municipal grid, eat heavy AEGIS aggression, industrial fabricator endgame, high power/high threat; DROVERS — the low-watt path: draft animals, biofuel, cultivated hedge-wall defenses, working WITH the Greenwave, minimal AEGIS contact, slow but nearly entropy-free; TIDEWRIGHTS — amphibious raid economy: barges and tidal generators, a floating base that migrates with the flood seasons, stripping AEGIS nodes for parts to trade over the dial rather than coexisting with the grid at all. Each reaches a strong endgame; none passes through the others' checkpoints.

### Goal 7 — beauty pays rent

Two native systems, both derived from the antagonists rather than a bolted-on beauty score. (1) CULTIVATED ECOLOGY: the Greenwave doesn't fight tended ecology — it flows around it. Planted hedgerows redirect vine growth like levees redirect water; mowed meadow strips act as firebreaks; green roofs stop root-heave on structures; a daylighted stream with a heron perch controls the rodents that gnaw ground cable; pollinator gardens buff greenhouse yield; herds graze peacefully through parkland instead of stampeding fences. A base that garlands its machines with gardens pays a fraction of the entropy tax and provokes less fauna — pure concrete-and-razor-wire works, but bleeds maintenance forever. (2) STANDARD RUNS: cable and pipe hung properly — snapped to restored pylons, spaced, labeled conduit, junction boxes at grade — can be maintained by reflashed drones; ground spaghetti can't (drones only service standardized runs), gets overgrown, gnawed, and flood-shorted. So 'tidy' is literally the precondition for automating your own upkeep. The two together mean the mechanically optimal base converges on the solarpunk pastoral-industrial look — orderly lines threading through deliberate greenery — and it's fun to build because both systems are placement puzzles (routing growth, snapping runs), not a score to appease. Beauty falls out of playing well, which is exactly the Factorio-main-bus phenomenon, aimed at gardens.

### Goal 8 — why you're doing this

Outer frame: SIGNAL ARCADIA — a shortwave broadcast from a stratospheric relay that passes overhead on a fixed real-time cadence, carrying fragments from a surviving enclave and a standing invitation: build the Long Ear (a grid-hungry transmitter array), answer, and eventually light a corridor for contact. Not a quest chain — a web of three mechanisms that cascade to the current minute: (1) THE DIAL — scanning frequencies finds voices, each anchored to a real place on YOUR map (a lighthouse keeper who wants her lamp relit, a factory caretaker AI negotiating for its own restoration, a drover clan trading beasts for medicine); their asks are generated from live map state, teach recipes, and conflict with each other, so 'story' is faction navigation, not a rail. (2) THE FLYOVER — during each overhead pass, if your mast network is lit and your grid holds steady load through the window, you receive a payload drop and the next narrative fragment; this converts story directly into engineering ('we need 40kW stable at 3 a.m. Sunday' is a narrative deadline that is also a grid problem). (3) THE LEDGER — the server's shared night map, where every restored district glows; the fantasy is turning the lights back on, and the answer to 'why am I clearing these vines?' is always visible: clearing vines → keeps the line live → keeps the mast lit → catches Sunday's flyover → answers Arcadia. Every moment-to-moment task sits on that one chain, and the chain has no fixed order of links.

## Multiplayer (2–20, persistent)

2-20 on a persistent server maps cleanly onto the setting's native jobs: linefolk (splice, route, expand), tenders (farms, cultivated ecology, pharmacology), wranglers (fauna taming, herd management), raiders (AEGIS strips, black-box recovery), and the radio operator/quartermaster running the dial and faction ledger — small groups just wear multiple hats. Offline: your reflashed drones, steadings, and automation keep running, and the Greenwave keeps growing — but the key balancing valve is that pressure scales with RESTORED FOOTPRINT and GRID LOAD, not headcount, so a server whose players go quiet can deliberately MOTHBALL districts: a clean shutdown (drained lines, greased switchgear, tarped panels) that freezes a district cheaply for later re-wake, versus letting it rot on the vine. AEGIS responds to grid activity, so a dark base is not raided offline — storms still bite, which keeps returning players with something to fix but nothing unfairly lost. Pressure scales with players indirectly and honestly: more players → more ambition → more lit footprint → more entropy frontier and AEGIS response; flyover payload volume also scales with active heads so small crews aren't starved. Griefing/overlap is softened by the topology: the grid is a shared commons (your splice helps everyone downstream), which makes cooperation the default physics of the world rather than a social rule.

## Closest existing games

- Factorio — belts drawn on blank land vs. found topology you splice, defend, and study; pollution aggro becomes grid-load aggro
- Timberborn — shares hydrology-as-core-threat, but colony-sim puppeteering vs. embodied avatars in real-time co-op
- Terra Nil — shares the restoration fantasy, but as a tidy puzzle; here restoration is contested, taxed, and survival-priced
- Pacific Drive — shares hostile-zone-with-old-tech dread, but run-based roguelite vs. one persistent shared world
- Don't Starve Together — shares relentless co-op survival pressure, but with a deep automation stack instead of a crafting plateau

## Designer-admitted risks

- The entropy tax is the whole game's spine and its biggest tedium risk: if vine-clearing counterplay isn't genuinely strategic (routing, ecology, drones), the game degenerates into weeding simulator; the pressure/chore tuning is a knife edge and will eat months of playtesting.
- Found-topology automation trades away Factorio's blank-canvas creativity: players who love greenfield layout may feel railroaded by pre-baked ruins, and procedurally generating plausible, READABLE ruined-city grids in isometric 2D is a hard, unglamorous content-gen problem the whole design leans on.
- Server simulation cost: persistent hydrology, vegetation regrowth, fauna migration, and grid-load simulation for a 20-player world on small cloud VMs demands aggressive tick-LOD (dormant-chunk freezing, mothball states); if the sim gets coarsened too far, the 'living world' pillar quietly dies.
- No tech tree risks onboarding fog and wiki-meta: schematics-in-places can dead-end a map or funnel every server toward the known-best scavenge route; the working-reference fallback and dial hints must be strong enough that a new player always sees a next step without ever seeing a checklist.
- The double-edged grid could produce a dominant dark-side strategy: if AEGIS response outweighs industrial payoff, everyone plays low-watt Drovers and the setting's signature fantasy (relighting the world) becomes the trap option — asymmetric-archetype balance plus the offline-safety rule (AEGIS ignores dark bases) pull against the 'constant pressure' pillar and must be reconciled carefully.

**Biggest weakness.** The entire design load-bears on procedurally generating coherent, readable, plausibly-ruined infrastructure with genuine electrical/rail/water topology — a content-generation research problem the pitch names in its risks but drastically underprices. If the generator produces samey or illegible grids (the normal small-team outcome), goals 3, 4, and 6 collapse simultaneously into a wiki-documented single route per map archetype, and found topology becomes strictly worse than Factorio's blank canvas: all of the constraint, none of the variety.

**How a real server dies (failure scenario).** Hour 14, three players on a persistent server, week two. The generator put the only surviving substation cluster on the river bend — same as the last two maps, and the wiki's 'Marsh Opening' guide already covers the optimal splice route, so the 'no research screen' discovery fantasy is dead on arrival. The nightly Greenwave tax on their restored footprint has become a fixed 35-minute machete chore, because the vine-cutter crawlers that would automate it require capturing a Shepherd, and Shepherds only wake at grid loads the crew can't yet defend — a dependency deadlock the sacrifice economy presents as 'choice.' Tuesday, two of them log in to find the weekend storm flooded the pump district while everyone was offline; the fix is manual sandbagging, the exact chore L2 was supposed to kill. They run the numbers, mothball every district — which the game rewards with near-zero AEGIS response and near-zero entropy — keep a dark camp that meets all survival needs cheaply, and realize the mechanically rational endpoint is refusing to play the game's own fantasy. The server goes quiet by Friday.

**The steelman fix.** Replace freeform procgen with a hand-authored district-chunk grammar: 30–50 handcrafted city-block templates with typed connector sockets for grid, rail, and water, recombined per map WFC-style, with only hydrology, AEGIS placement, and fauna routes fully procedural. This guarantees readable, topologically sane ruins (rescuing goals 3, 4, and 6 at once) while preserving map-driven strategy variance, and shrinks the pitch's riskiest work item by an order of magnitude. Pair it with one non-elective clock — the relay's orbit decays, so the flyover window closes on a server-wide schedule — to kill the dominant dark-turtle strategy.

**Distinctiveness (4/5).** Closest game: Factorio. Resuscitating found topology under an ecological and security immune response genuinely inverts Factorio's blank-canvas-plus-biters formula and no shipped title occupies that space (ECO and Terra Nil each hold one adjacent piece), but the difference only matters if the generator produces topology worth finding — otherwise it regresses to Factorio-with-worse-canvas.

**Feasibility (2/5).** The expensive core is the coupled server-side simulations — district hydrology, per-tile vegetation regrowth, fauna migration, and electrical load-flow — running persistently for 2–20 players in real time on small VMs, layered under the procgen-ruins problem the design leans on. The 'Eastward/Ghibli-decay' hand-painted register is one of the most labor-intensive art bars in indie 2D and is casually invoked, and the three archetype endgames (rail, animal logistics, barges/floating bases) roughly triple content scope. This is a 4–6 year scope pitched as a small-team browser game; shipping requires cutting about half of it.
