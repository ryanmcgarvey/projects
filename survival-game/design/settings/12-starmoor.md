# 12. STARMOOR

> Round 2 (player-seeded) · Seed: `ship + mobile station` · Goal fit **29/40** · Distinctiveness **4/5** · Feasibility **3/5** · Verdict: **promising**

**Pitch.** A co-op survival-automation game about the last free city in a burned-out star cluster: every player flies a nimble, cheap-to-replace ship, but everyone shares one slow, precious station — a freeport that feeds on whatever it's moored to and gets easier to find every day it stays. You mine, trade, board, and recruit to spin a logistics web around each mooring; then, when the rock runs thin and the predators' fix on your position hardens, you stow the city, light the burn spine, and drag your entire civilization to the next anchorage — and rebuild the web around wherever you land.

**The hook.** A base-building game where the base must periodically flee its own success: your logistics empire has a lifespan by design, and the loudest, most dangerous thing you will ever do is move home.

**Tone.** Clean 2D top-down vector-and-glow — dark navigation charts, thin lit trade-lanes, ships as readable silhouettes, and the station a warm lattice of windows and promenade light against the dust; Highfleet's radar romance crossed with harbor-town coziness. Emotionally: the warmth of a port at night inside a migratory melancholy — every home is temporary, which is exactly why the crew lights it well.

## How it serves the founding goals

### Goal 1 — constant pressure

Two pushes and three predators, none of which expire. Push one, THE TAPER: the station cannot float free — it moors to a resource body (a comet core, a ferrite swarm, a hulk field) and drinks from it, and every mooring's yield curve declines continuously from the day you clamp on; there is no mooring that lasts forever, only a choice about how far down the curve you ride. Push two, THE FIX: everything you operate emits on three channels — **glare** (heat and light from industry), **wake** (drive plumes and lane traffic), **chatter** (radio, market broadcasts) — and the cluster's powers integrate those emissions into an ever-better firing solution on your position. The Fix is footprint, not clock: a quiet skeleton station accrues it slowly, a roaring twelve-lane port accrues it fast, and only a Weigh (moving the city) resets it. Between taper falling and Fix rising, "stay forever" is a strategy with a visible, worsening price — the solved-base state cannot exist because the base's context is always decaying under it. The predators escalate on three orthogonal channels with distinct counterplay (this is deliberate armor against single-currency meta convergence): **the Combine**, a tariff cartel that wants you *paying* — tolls, lane tithes, blockades, keyed to your wealth and traffic; **the Breakers**, a shipbreaker armada that follows depletion and wants you as *salvage* — raids and boarding attempts keyed to the scrap-value of your footprint; and **the Hush**, a zealot navy that enforces dark skies and wants you *quiet* — strikes keyed purely to your glare and chatter. All three read the Fix; each reads a different channel hardest. Threats tax tempo, never progress (Rule 1): ships lost become escape pods home and a reprint from stocks in minutes, with the cargo lootable at the wreck; the station is never destroyed — sieges knock modules offline for repair and tithe stores. And the loop breathes (Rule 3): tolls are posted, grievances are inspectable, and the Fix forecast on the Chart Table tells you roughly when each predator can next reach you, so lulls are real and plannable — the tension is a tide chart, not a fire alarm.

### Goal 2 — the automation layers

Tier-0 is deliberately hands-on and deliberately finite: you personally hold the cutter beam to a seam, fly every load in your own hold, dock and unload crate by crate, patch hull plates, man the turret during a shakedown, and haggle with each passing kithship (the neutral free-trader family caravans). Six systems retire those chores — bought in any order, at any depth, from the same stocks that keep the city breathing; they are purchases on a continuous curve, not a sequence, and a reckless crew could rush any of them in hour two. **Stevedore drones** kill crate-hauling at the dock; they draw reactor power and are the first thing pirates pinch during a raid. **Claim rigs** (outposts) kill holding the cutter beam — auto-extractors lashed to a body, running as extraction rates into silos; they create defense surface and a haul-away demand (a full silo halts the rig). **Freight lanes** kill pack-muling — drone freighters on scheduled runs between claims, station, and markets; every lane is exposure, subject to piracy tithe, and its wake feeds the Fix. **Crew** kill the station's interior chores — each recruit is a named specialist who retires a specific job you've personally done to resentment: a bosun (hull patching), a gunner (turret watch), a factor (market runs), a wright (the reprint queue). The anti-treadmill contract is strict: crew never generate tasks — they consume rations and air as small flat rates from stocks your automation already fills, and if stocks run dry they don't die or scream, they stop working and lounge until fed; headcount is capped by berths built, and each hire must retire more attention than their rate costs, so the ledger nets positive by construction (Rule 5). **Pickets** kill flying escort duty yourself — armed patrol drones on lanes and approaches that beat the piracy tithe down; they eat ammunition and fuel logistics, and arming your lanes raises your *belligerence* profile, converting Combine tolls into Combine ambushes. **Compacts** kill the standing market-run — negotiated supply agreements with kithships and factions that auto-price and auto-deliver; they create obligations with teeth (an under-supplied compact becomes a logged grievance) and their chatter feeds the Fix. Everything above reduces to rate arithmetic — extraction rates, lane throughputs, tithe percentages, ration draw (Rule 14) — which is what makes the offline fast-forward an integral, not a replay. Why this is fresh against Factorio's belts: the web is drawn on a chart, not a floor — every link is an exposed lane with predator-shaped costs, throughput's co-cost is signature, and the entire graph is positional relative to a station that periodically *moves*, so logistics here is a portfolio of bets with lifespans, not permanent infrastructure.

### Goal 2 — the sacrifice economy

One economy, no research currency (Rule 4): **ice** (refines to burn-stock, air, and water), **ferrite** (plate, hulls, rigs), **isotopes** (reactor fuel, warheads), **biomass** (rations via hydroponics), **longsteel** (pre-collapse alloy, salvage-only, for high-grade spines and cold plates) — plus four meta-pools everything competes for: reactor watts, hull volume, crew-hours, and signature budget. The Weigh itself is the great sacrifice sink: a burn leg costs on the order of 2,000 burn-stock — about three full days of refinery output — plus stowage (production offline for the transit, unstowed fragile modules take damage), plus the invalidation of every lane you rigged, and the burn is the brightest thing you will ever emit, so the whole cluster knows the city is moving and heavy. Worked decision, MINUTE 30: you've hand-mined 40 ice from the mooring's frost seam and the station's scrubbers draw constantly. (a) Refine it all to 20 burn-stock — the move fund's first deposit, pure future safety; (b) feed the hydroponic galleries — three days of rations, enough to sign the two riggers idling at the dock, each of whom retires a chore you're already sick of; (c) trade it to the kithship on approach for a used stevedore drone — kills dock-hauling today. One pool, three futures, and whichever two chores you don't kill stay on your hands through the next raid cycle. Worked decision, HOUR 20: the mooring has tapered to 60% of week-one yield; the Combine's fix is good enough that they've posted a toll gate skimming 15% off your main ore lane; a Breaker tender has been sighted twice. (a) Pay — predictable −15%, peace holds, the Fix keeps climbing; (b) spend the 30 isotopes earmarked for the second reactor on armed pickets — tithe falls to ~5%, but belligerence flips future tolls into ambushes; (c) re-lane through the dust shoal — tithe near zero and your wake damps so the Fix actually *decays*, but throughput drops 40%; (d) call the Weigh three weeks early on a 70% burn reserve — arrive nearly dry at the next mooring, betting everything on the prospector's ice report. Every option converts one kind of security into another; there is no "just wait."

### Goal 4 — the map changes the plan

The generator deals each *leg*, not just each server: mooring types and their yield mixes (comet cores: ice-rich, structurally fragile; ferrite swarms: metal-rich, dry; hulk fields: practice- and longsteel-rich, claimed and infested), dust shoals (natural glare/wake damping — quiet country), the local star's strength (feeds hydroponics and the thrive-light of the station), faction claim geography, kithship route density, and the bearing-and-quality of rumored next moorings — and the generator always denies something (Rule 10). Because the city moves, the map re-deals mid-campaign forever. Examples: (1) Dealt an ice-poor ferrite cluster — fuel is the bottleneck, so a fat burn reserve is unaffordable; the strategy becomes a long-stay Grand Port leg: build allure, court kithships, buy ice on compacts — which means staying sweet with the Combine and eating tolls, while your ferrite wealth makes you the Breakers' dream prize, so plate and point-defense before comfort. (2) Dealt a mooring inside a dust shoal — dark running is free, the Hush can't see you and mostly won't come; skip the signal mast, run a skeleton crew, prospect wide — but the shoal starves your hydroponics of starlight, so rations come from kithship compacts or raided Breaker larders, and the leg's whole shape is "quiet, hungry, sneaky." (3) Dealt a graveyard cluster, Breaker-claimed — a knowledge rush: strip longsteel and shipwright practices fast, every dive logging grievance, so this leg has an expiry from day one and you plan the Weigh before you've finished mooring, aiming to leave with a fat practice book and thin stores.

### Goal 5 — base ⇄ combat loop

Station → combat: (1) a fleet is a rate — the print yard's depth and hull stocks set how fast losses are replaced, so your industrial base literally *is* your combat endurance, and a lost ship is a supply question, not a tragedy; (2) the refinery chain makes magrail slugs and cold plates, so your ability to fight — and to fight *dark* — is manufactured at home; (3) during a station siege the reactor budget is shared: browning out the refinery to feed the point-defense ring mid-fight is a base-design decision made under fire, and crew gunners you recruited (and housed well) man the escort freighters. Combat/exploration → station: (1) practices and charts are the loot — dissect a Hush coldjet at the yard to learn quiet-running, take a Combine ledger-safe for lane charts and mooring surveys that feed the next Weigh; (2) boarding a pirate hull frees its pressed crew, and freed crew are your best recruits — recruitment is downstream of combat, not a hiring menu; (3) grapnel-tow a cleared hulk home and it becomes a module shell or the seed of an outpost — territory as loot. No self-sufficient turtles: hydroponics need starlight and compacts need the outside; no baseless nomads: every slug, plate, and reprint traces to the city (Rule 11).

### Goals 3 + 6 — many paths, no milestones

No ship classes, no tech eras. Three systems replace them. (1) THE CONTINUOUS HULL CURVE: the yard prints hulls along smooth tradeoff curves — mass buys hardpoints and hold but costs fuel, agility, and signature — so "bigger" is a position on a curve with real downsides, not a ladder rung; a heavy hauler is *worse* at running dark and slipping shoals, forever. (2) PRACTICES, NOT NODES: shipwright practices are knowledge-as-loot, learned by dissecting real machines — wrecks, salvage, boarded prizes — and every practice is sideways (Rule 9): the coldjet drive trades thrust for silence, the decoy caster trades hold space for a fake Fix signature, the grapnel rig trades a weapon mount for boarding and towing; nothing is "Mk2." (3) PRICE-RISK-DISTANCE GATES (Rule 7): anything is legal on day one — a new pilot can fly to the graveyard and strip a longsteel spine off a wreck in hour one if they dare the Breakers; it's expensive, dangerous, and occasionally brilliant, which is world logic, not a quest flag. Even the Weigh is unscheduled: when to move, whether to move, whether to never move — continuous choices on visible curves, different on every server. Viable archetypes the tuning must protect: **the Grand Port** (high-glare, beautiful, trade-magnet city that pays tolls as a cost of business and moves rarely and ceremonially); **the Quiet Hulk** (dark-running skeleton city hopping dust shoals, earning by prospecting and gray trade, starving the Fix); **the Corsair Flotilla** (light station, heavy ships — wealth from raiding convoys and selling protection, belligerence maxed, trade poor); **the Web** (outpost-heavy claim empire with maximal lanes and maximal surface — the logistics-engineering game). The map decides which is strong per leg, and mixing per-leg is the expected meta.

### Goal 7 — beauty pays rent

Two world-logic mechanisms, no beauty meter, both computed from the working station's emergent properties (Rule 12). (1) THRIVE: settlers and crew read their city — starlight reach (lightwells and galleries with actual sightlines to the local star, which the map varies), volume per berth, green adjacency (hydroponics as amenity, not just food), noise gradient (distance from refinery and yard), and promenade circulation (can a settler walk a loop without crossing industry). Thrive pays through the systems everything else already uses: crew work speed, recruit arrival rate and quality (kithships carry gossip — the lit city gets walk-ons at the dock), and settler *retention* after hardship — after a siege or a hard Weigh, the high-thrive city holds its people; the windowless box watches them ship out with the next caravan. (2) ALLURE: visiting kithship frequency and prices read the approach view — the lit promenade, ordered docking slips, a landmark silhouette. Beauty is priced as a genuine line item: galleries, lightwells, and promenades cost plate, hull volume, watts, and — because windows are glare — signature; it competes with industry for everything. And it must not strictly dominate or be dominated: the Quiet Hulk deliberately forgoes thrive and allure and is paid in darkness and a light ration bill; the Grand Port commits to them and is paid in people and prices. Because the scoring is composable properties rather than a template, unlimited layouts score well — and because the city periodically moves, your beautiful build is re-staged against a new starfield every leg, which is the marketing screenshot engine.

### Goal 8 — why you're doing this

The frame: the cluster's empires burned each other out generations ago; what's left are company fleets, breaker armadas, zealot navies, and family caravans — and one freeport, yours, the last neutral harbor, a city that survives by never being where the powers finally agree to divide it. The outer goal is the city itself: keep it free, keep it fed, keep it growing — every mooring a season, every Weigh a chapter, with rumored **deep moorings** (stable pre-collapse anchorages, defensible, half-mythical) as optional crew-chosen destinations; whether a server hunts one or stays migratory forever is its story, not a campaign terminus. The why-chain walks cleanly (Rule 13): why mine ice? → to fill the burn reserve → so the city can weigh anchor before the yield dies and the predators' fix hardens → so the last free port stays free. Four mechanisms cascade from frame to the current minute, all inspectable simulated state, all server-shared: **the Chart Table** (the mooring's live yield curve, the Fix forecast per faction, the burn reserve, rumor bearings from looted charts — the group's self-organizing to-do list); **the Ledger** (every automation flow as visible rates — what's earning, what's leaking, what the tithe took); **the Grievance Book** (every faction relation as logged causes — you can always see *why* the Hush is coming and what would appease them); **the Census** (settlers arriving and leaving — the city's opinion of your stewardship, rendered as people). Nobody asks why they're smelting; they're filling the tanks that move the city that everyone can see.

## Multiplayer (2–20, persistent)

One freeport per server: the station is the server's shared crown jewels, every player owns a personal nimble ship (reprintable in minutes from communal stocks), and personal outposts and lanes live inside the shared web. Roles emerge from overload rather than classes (Rule 6): pilots and prospectors work the frontier, wardens run pickets and sieges, keepers build the city and sign the crew, brokers tune lanes, tolls, and compacts — and the task list is structurally never empty because every automation layer's new demand (defense surface, lane exposure, obligations, belligerence) lands on it. **Offline contract, concretely (Rule 2):** production continues as fast-forwarded rates; piracy resolves as bounded percentage tithes on exposed lanes (rate math set by your picket quality and lane routing — automation quality is the offline game); faction pressure accrues as visible siege and grievance meters; blockades can bottle a claim's output but never breach it; destruction only lands against present defenders. Logging off is never optimal — your web keeps earning while it keeps emitting, so you return richer and more charted, which is a decision, not a punishment. Pressure scales to presence and footprint: the lone Tuesday-night player faces a two-skiff shakedown, not a Breaker tender with escorts — and has a full solo menu: fly an escort shift, run a prospecting survey for the next mooring, dive a wreck for practices, re-lane around a toll, sign and house a recruit. **Day-30 join (Rule 16):** practices are city-shared (no personal research track), a ship prints in minutes, and frontier verbs never depreciate — the newcomer's first hour is escorting the ore lane, spotting a Breaker scout on the Chart Table, and getting thanked for it. **Governance and blast radius (Rule 15):** every placement, removal, and compact is logged and attributed at the Chart Table; scuttle-scale actions carry posted delays; and the Weigh is proposed at the table and fires after a posted countdown unless objected — asynchronous consent, so a two-player server can move the city and a twenty-player server can argue about it, but no one wakes up mid-transit without having seen it coming.

## Closest existing games

- Starsector — colonies, factions, fleet logistics, but single-player, tiered hulls, no persistence contract
- EVE Online — territory, piracy, movable citadels, but MMO-scale spreadsheet warfare, not friends-scale survival-crafting with bounded loss
- Highfleet — signature/emissions warfare and a ruinously expensive moving home, but an authored solo campaign
- Factorio: Space Age — automation depth across space, but belts on safe static ground; here the logistics graph is exposed, priced, and periodically re-dealt by the moving base
- Barotrauma / Void Crew — co-op crewed-vessel tension, but no economy, colony, or persistent world

## Designer-admitted risks

- The Fix is rubber-banding wearing a trench coat if tuned lazily — the exact critique that hit Galeheart's radiance. It needs sub-linear scaling with real counterplay (decoy casters, quiet legs, dust shoals, misinformation) so growth is taxed but never strictly self-defeating; get it wrong and this is anti-Factorio feel-bad.
- The Weigh lives on a knife-edge in both directions: too cheap and it's a solved ritual checklist by the third move; too painful and servers turtle into the Grand Port forever and the heart of the seed dies. The generator has to re-deal each leg hard enough that arrival is a genuinely new problem, which makes procgen quality load-bearing.
- Offline tithes must read as weather, not theft. The math complies with Rule 2, but if the returning player's ledger says "−18% while you slept" without legible cause and counterplay, it *feels* like offline destruction; the entire persistence contract lives in that UX.
- Three faction pressure channels plus an economy plus live combat is a lot of sim. Feasibility depends on factions being reputational rate-systems that spawn encounters — not simulated fleets — and the fakery may show at the edges (players tailing a "patrol" to its nonexistent home port).
- Thrive scoring must genuinely admit unlimited layouts, or a wiki berth-ring becomes the one true city and Goal 3 dies in the promenade; and shared-city governance (who calls the Weigh, who spends the reserve) is softened by asynchronous consent but unsolved for a twenty-player server split into two camps.

**Biggest weakness.** The Weigh — the entire hook — is strictly dominated for the winning build. Rule 1's bounded loss guarantees the station can never die, and compacts let any input be imported, so a wealthy Grand Port pays the Fix as a toll line-item and neutralizes the taper with purchased ice: staying costs a 15% skim you outgrow, while moving costs three days of output, every rigged lane, and the brightest emission in the game. The pitch's own HOUR-20 worked example and its own leg-1 map example ("ice-poor: buy ice on compacts, eat tolls") jointly teach players the turtle build the designer swears the tuning will prevent.

**How a real server dies (failure scenario).** Six friends, 4-hour Tuesday sessions. Hours 0–20 are excellent — the minute-30 and hour-20 decisions land exactly as written. Week 2: they're wealthy, the broker signs an ice compact, taper is now a solved line on the Ledger; the Combine toll (15%) is compared at the Chart Table against a 2,000-burn-stock Weigh and loses forever. Week 3: the Hush strikes at their promenade glare — but sieges only knock modules offline and tithe stores, so it's repaired in twenty minutes and logged as weather; meanwhile a full silo halted a claim rig overnight, the under-fed compact logged a grievance nobody online could have prevented, and absence has quietly become negligence. Week 5 they finally call a Weigh for the story of it — and notice destruction only lands against present defenders, so they set the countdown for 4 a.m. and log off: the loudest, most dangerous act in the game resolves as an unwatched offline fast-forward. Arrival is re-rigging the identical star-topology web against different ore ratios; two players call leg 2 "homework" and quit; by week 7 the server is permanently moored, paying rent, and the last free city is a strip mall with a toll booth.

**The steelman fix.** Close the import loophole: make one station-critical input moor-bound and un-importable — the anchor-feed the city literally drinks, which no compact can ship — so every mooring has a true horizon that wealth can defer but never erase. Then pay the Weigh forward instead of only punishing it: first-anchor salvage rights, virgin-yield spikes, and fresh charts on arrival, so moving is the loop's payoff rather than its penalty box — and resolve the transit as a presence-scaled set-piece so it can't be optimally slept through.

**Distinctiveness (4/5).** Closest game: Starsector. The parts are borrowed — Highfleet's signature warfare, Starsector's colonies and factions, Emberdeep's own three-channel emissions — but the migrating shared city is a genuinely new spine nobody ships, and it differs enough to matter *if and only if* the Weigh survives the economics above.

**Feasibility (3/5).** Credit where due: rates-not-items is native, factions are meters, and there is no physics bet — this is the only pitch in two rounds that did the Rule 14 homework up front. The expensive bite is that it's three games in one client — a chart-scale economy sim, a station interior with sightline/circulation/noise scoring (a second spatial engine), and real-time browser-netcode ship combat with boarding — and the faction fakery's seams (patrols with no home port) are admitted, not solved.
