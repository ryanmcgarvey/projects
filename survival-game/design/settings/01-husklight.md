# 1. HUSKLIGHT

> Seed: `ship` · Goal fit **36/40** · Distinctiveness **4/5** · Feasibility **2/5** · Verdict: **promising**

**Pitch.** A real-time co-op survival-automation game aboard a centuries-derelict generation ship where the base IS the ship: you splice its dying power grid, reawaken half-mad subsystem AIs, and reflash feral maintenance drones to keep ten thousand cryosleeping colonists alive. There is no factory to build from scratch — only a broken megastructure to triage, and every watt you spend automating your life is a watt stolen from someone's cryopod.

**The hook.** Your factory is a dying megastructure that argues back — you automate by reawakening ancient systems with their own agendas, and the power bill is measured in sleeping human lives.

**Tone.** Flashlight beams in cathedral-dark corridors: an ocean-liner-in-space built art-deco beautiful, now buried under rust, frost, and bioluminescent overgrowth — restoring the ship literally scrapes grime off brass trim. Top-down 2D with dynamic lighting and line-of-sight (both are core mechanics anyway), chunky readable sprites over vector deck plans; emotionally it is lighthouse-keeper melancholy, not horror — you are small, and the ship is a dying god you nurse.

## How it serves the founding goals

### Goal 1 — constant pressure

Three interlocking pressures, none of which can be "solved." (1) THE DIMMING: the reactor's output decays on a real-time curve — the ship's total joules are finite. Powered space needs watts for light, heat, and air circulation; unpowered sectors go dark, freeze, depressurize, and get colonized. Every expansion raises your power bill against a shrinking supply, so brownout triage never ends; late-game you run a huge fragile grid where one failed splice cascades. (2) ENTROPY EVENTS: hull lice ('rustmites') chew micro-breaches, seals age, conduits short — a systemic failure simulator (not a spawn timer) generates leaks and faults weighted by the age and condition of what you've restored, so a bigger base means more live alarms. (3) SUCCESS ATTRACTS THE VERDANCE: the feral bio-decks grow toward heat, light, and CO2 — your warm corridors, your running Foundry, even your breathing players are bait, so thriving pulls the jungle and its fauna toward you (a built-in anti-turtle). Pressure stays interesting rather than chore-like because responses are strategic, not rote: you can patch a leak, reroute around it, assign a Custodian to it, vent and abandon the sector, or jettison the whole section — and because the pressure sources trade against each other (running the Foundry hot to fix the grid feeds the Verdance; pruning the Verdance costs suit time you need for hull patrol).

### Goal 2 — the automation layers

The metaphor: you never place belts on a blank floor — you REAWAKEN a pre-existing, procedurally broken ship, and its systems have agendas. Tier-0 manual life: hand-carrying salvage on a sled, hand-cranking dead airlocks, patching leaks with sealant guns, burning lithium candles to scrub your own CO2, hand-pruning Verdance off conduits, standing watch with a flashlight. Layers: (1) GRID SPLICING — repair conduits, add splice boxes and breakers to route reactor watts; alleviates hand-carried batteries and lanterns, enables everything else. (2) TUBE & TRAM RESTORATION — the ship has a legacy pneumatic tube network and cargo tram spine; repair found segments and stations to move materials; alleviates sled-hauling. Fresh vs Factorio: logistics is routing through FOUND topology — you negotiate with the network the map generated, bridge severed trunks, and fight over trunk lines, rather than paving optimal belts anywhere. (3) CUSTODIAN REFLASHING — capture rogue maintenance drones (Sweepers, Welders, Primes) in a Flash Cradle and author their priority queues (patch leaks > prune vines > haul to Foundry); alleviates patrol, repair, and hauling labor. They keep deep firmware you can't override — every Custodian on the ship, yours included, drops everything to answer a hull-breach klaxon. (4) FABRICATOR AWAKENING — the Foundry (plate/parts, vents heat), the Autochef (food from biomass), the Loom (filters, suit liners) are giant fixed machines woken with watts, parts, and feedstock rations; alleviates hand-crafting, but each has appetites and side effects you site your base around. (5) BIO-DECK DOMESTICATION — grow-lattices and pruning drones turn feral decks into farms that make food AND scrub CO2, replacing lithium candles; alleviates the entire atmosphere chore if you accept living beside the jungle. (6) SUBSYSTEM AI SHARDS — wake the Gardener (atmosphere/biology), the Harbormaster (tram scheduling), the Immune System (defense turrets and white-cell drones): whole-domain automation from entities with centuries-old priorities you manage, bribe, and occasionally fight. The freshness across all six: your automation is agents and negotiated systems embedded in found architecture, not tiles you own outright — it can revert, argue, and surprise you.

### Goal 2 — the sacrifice economy

Four currencies the survival loop also needs. WATTS: a hard, visibly declining budget — waking the Foundry draws power that could light corridors or run cryo-bay chillers. PLATE & SEALANT: the same hull steel patches a breach or builds a machine arm; the same polymer seals a leak or gaskets a tube station. TERRITORY: you can deliberately cut power to a deck (it decays), vent it (kills its problems and its value), or JETTISON a whole section for a one-time windfall of plate and a permanent drop in grid load — sacrificing map, sleepers housed there, and any tram routes through it, forever. SLEEPER RISK: cryo-bays draw constant watts and have hour-scale buffer; browning one out doesn't kill instantly, it raises decay risk on a visible ledger — so under-investing in survival has a body count. Worked example, MINUTE 30: you hold a two-room pressurized toehold and have 2 sealant charges. A micro-breach is hissing in the corridor to the Autochef (1 charge now, or it grows into a 4-plate rupture), your suit's O2 recharge line is also cracked (1 charge), and you spotted a sealant cache one dark sector away — reachable by 10 minutes of hand-cranked airlocks past a probable vent-crawler nest. Patch the O2 line and eat cold rations; patch the food corridor and live on lithium candles you also need for your first Custodian flash; or gamble the run. Worked example, HOUR 20: reactor rod 3 of 6 is failing — 18% output drop in two hours. (a) Fabricate a replacement: 40 refined plate plus an hour of the Foundry running hot, whose heat bloom will pull a Verdance surge into the district. (b) Brown out Deck 11 — Bay 11-C's 800 sleepers have ~30 hours of buffer — and race to jettison the dead aft section for salvage that funds the rod with margin, severing the aft tram loop your hauling depends on. (c) Wake the Harbormaster shard you found last week: it load-balances the grid 'for free,' but reserves 30% of tram throughput for its ancient ceremonial schedule unless you keep bribing its queue. The decision hurts well because every option mortgages a different future, and the Ledger shows you exactly who's downstream.

### Goal 4 — the map changes the plan

The generator varies deck archetypes and adjacency (Foundry district, Hydroponic Ring, Cryo Vaults, Reactor Spine, vacuum-breached scars), the found tram/tube topology, conduit trunk routes, where each fabricator and AI shard sleeps, breach patterns, and where the Verdance already holds. Examples: (1) The only intact Foundry spawned three decks past a hard vacuum breach — instead of the usual repressurize-and-move-in, we ran power to it remotely and reflashed vacuum-immune Custodians to operate it unmanned, becoming a drone-labor economy two hours in. (2) Cryo vaults came scattered across five decks instead of one block — fortress play is off the table, so we restored the tram spine first and built a federation of micro-bases with a rolling repair crew. (3) The reactor spine generated flush against a feral bio-deck — its waste heat makes Verdance pressure unwinnable by pruning, so we woke the Gardener early, conceded it two decks, and pivoted to a bio-economy (oxylung moss, sealant-sap, Autochef biomass) we hadn't planned to touch. (4) A prior jettison scar severed the tram trunk mid-ship — we built a vacuum cargo catapult across the gap rather than the 40-plate detour corridor.

### Goal 5 — base ⇄ combat loop

Base to combat: (1) an awakened Foundry + Loom turn salvage into real kit — arc-welders repurposed as beam weapons, riot-foam launchers that immobilize Custodians for capture, reinforced suit liners for vacuum fights; (2) restored infrastructure is a weapon platform — tram-mounted turret bogies ride your own rails to a fight, and atmosphere control lets you vent a corridor to suffocate spore-hounds or overpressure-blast a nest, but only in sectors whose doors, pumps, and grid you've restored; (3) reflashed Custodian escorts and Immune System white-cells fight beside you where your registry coverage extends. Combat/exploration to base: (1) downing a Custodian Prime yields intact Priority Cores — new drone job classes and flash schematics you cannot fabricate; (2) burning out a bio-deck Root Node yields cultivar seed-stock (farmable strains: sealant-sap vines that bio-caulk small leaks, oxylung moss) plus the deck itself as territory; (3) raiding Stowaway camps (feral descendants of crew who refused cryo, deep in the dark decks) recovers jury-rig schematics — a whole low-watt, ugly-but-cheap parallel machine line — and their hand-drawn charts reveal dark-sector topology; (4) sleeper vault archives explored under escort yield crew Engrams, personal ability unlocks (splice mastery, drone whispering). The loop closes because the best salvage sits in exactly the hostile dark the base's power, air, and drones let you reach.

### Goals 3 + 6 — many paths, no milestones

There is no tech tree. Capability = which ship systems you've awakened plus which blueprints, Priority Cores, and Engrams you've physically found — and knowledge is scattered through the world by the generator, not arranged in a research order. Nothing is level-locked: any system can be attempted at any time, gated only by watts, parts, and danger, so 'progression' is a continuously improving condition/efficiency stat on everything you've restored (a limping Foundry at 30% condition is an early-game reality, not a tier). Genuinely different viable archetypes: (1) THE CITADEL — jettison and vent aggressively, tow cryopods into one dense district, run a tiny hyper-efficient microgrid with deep defense; small, safe, and salvage-rich but forfeits most of the map's machines. (2) THE RING RUNNERS — restore the tram spine first and keep half the ship barely-alive as a federation of outposts run by Custodian labor; logistics-brained, exposed everywhere, compounding returns. (3) THE GREEN SHIP — ally with the Gardener, let the Verdance in on purpose: bio-caulked hulls, moss-scrubbed air, low-watt Stowaway jury-rigs; nearly frees you from the reactor, at the price of fauna management and a partner with its own expansion agenda. (4) THE CONCORD — wake every AI shard fast and play administrator, ruling a ship that mostly runs itself but constantly disputes your priorities. Order of operations, base shape, and even which pressure you treat as primary differ across all four.

### Goal 7 — beauty pays rent

THE INSPECTION PROTOCOL. The ship was designed to naval-architecture standards — trim lines, lighting spec, clearance widths, labeled pipes, braced conduit — and the Custodian fleet still enforces them. Legacy inspector drones patrol and grade spaces against the ship's design grammar: compliant, 'shipshape' spaces are recognized as SHIP, and Custodians maintain them for free (repaint, re-lube, patch wear) and won't dismantle your machines there; sloppy spaces are classified as debris and get 'tidied' — your unbraced splice heap is salvage to them. The grammar itself is load-bearing, not cosmetic: crossed power/data lines arc, bundled cable heaps are where rustmites nest, spaced machines shed heat, and working light fixtures ARE your sensor grid — lit sightlines feed turret targeting and give alarms real signage-based locations. So a beautiful base is cheaper to maintain, harder to infest, better at seeing, and legible to both drones and teammates. Late-game you can AMEND THE STANDARD at a restored registry console — canonize your crew's own aesthetic into the building code, and the ancient machines will lovingly maintain that instead. It produces builds both functional and lovely because the pretty thing and the efficient thing are the same simulated object, and because a period art-deco design language gives players a strong style to riff within rather than a blank canvas.

### Goal 8 — why you're doing this

Outer frame: you are the Waking Watch — stewards revived by the ship's failing master voice (the Purser) to keep the ark and its ten thousand cryosleepers alive long enough to make the Deceleration Burn at the destination star, on a far horizon set by the reactor's finite joules. Four cascading mechanisms, none of them a quest chain: (1) THE LEDGER — a live, server-wide tally of sleepers alive/at-risk/lost; every subsystem traces to it, and the UI shows the stakes of the thing in your hand ('this conduit feeds Bay 7 — 212 sleepers'), so micro-tasks always have a visible why. (2) THE MANIFEST — the Purser broadcasts a prioritized work-order feed generated from live simulation state (breach on Deck 9, rod 3 degrading, Verdance at junction K-K), an emergent systemic to-do, never scripted; you are always free to triage it your own way, and ignoring items has consequences rather than fail-states. (3) THE BURN CLOCK — the decay curve makes hoarding a losing strategy and pushes exploration outward: dark sectors hold the fuel reserves, rod stock, and shipwright archives that bend the curve. (4) THE SLEEPERS THEMSELVES — their vault records, Engrams, and occasional wakings make the cargo people, not points; the grimmest decisions (brownouts, jettisons) are priced in names.

## Multiplayer (2–20, persistent)

2-20 players on a persistent server, fictionally framed as a watch rotation: logging off is returning to cryo — you literally become part of what the others protect, and your suit docks safely. Division of labor falls out of the systems, not classes: grid engineer, boatswain (hull patrol/patching), drone wrangler, tram-master, cultivator, warden — plus the Ledger gives the whole server one shared score. While players are offline the world keeps simulating at reduced intensity: automation runs, Custodians work their queues, but the event budget (breaches, surges, incursions) scales with online steward count, and reactor decay slows near-empty so a quiet week doesn't death-spiral the save. Pressure scales with population diegetically: more waking bodies means more heat, CO2, and suit-oxygen draw — which directly feeds Verdance growth toward inhabited decks — and more restored load on the grid; large crews naturally federate into district teams because the tram network and Amended Standards make territory legible. Small crews lean harder on Custodian labor and AI shards, which the sacrifice economy prices accordingly.

## Closest existing games

- Factorio — logistics through found ship topology, not free-placed belts
- Oxygen Not Included — real-time embodied co-op, repair not excavation
- Barotrauma — persistent base-ship you restore, no piloting loop
- RimWorld — pressure from systemic decay, not a narrator AI
- IXION — real-time avatar survival co-op, not a pausable managerial city-builder

## Designer-admitted risks

- Persistent decay vs. offline time is a knife-edge: scale pressure down too much and the server feels paused, too little and saves death-spiral overnight — the diegetic fixes (cryo logoff, event budgets) still need ugly invisible tuning, and low-population hours will expose it.
- Browser/server simulation cost: continuous power-heat-atmosphere network sim plus agent-based Custodians across a megastructure for 20 players on cheap hosting demands aggressive sector-level LOD (freezing dark decks), which directly conflicts with the promise that the ship keeps living and the Verdance keeps creeping while you're elsewhere.
- The Inspection Protocol could collapse into checklist-gaming — players min-maxing trim placement rather than building beautifully — and the Amend-the-Standard feature is exploitable (legislate the cheapest possible aesthetic); the grammar needs the depth of a real style guide, which is a large content and tooling investment.
- AI shards and Custodians that fight your intent are one bad UX pass away from reading as bugs: the game must constantly explain WHY the Gardener overgrew your corridor or your drone abandoned its queue, or the freshest system becomes the most rage-inducing.
- Tone hazard in the sleeper economy: pricing decisions in human lives is the design's spine, but it invites players to treat sleepers as batteries and jettisons as arbitrage — the Ledger must make triage feel weighty without either moralizing or becoming a grim spreadsheet; relatedly, no tech tree plus adversarial systems makes the first hour brutal for a browser audience that bounces in minutes.

**Biggest weakness.** The finite real-time decay curve is structurally incompatible with a persistent 2-20 player browser server: a strictly dying world gives every save a scripted expiry date, punishes exactly the casual drop-in players browsers attract, and the pitch's own tuning fixes (decay slows near-empty, event budget scales with online steward count) mean the flagship pressure mechanic is secretly a difficulty slider. Worse, because waking players' heat and CO2 attract the Verdance and events scale with headcount, the systems as written make logging off the mathematically optimal survival strategy — the design punishes presence in a genre whose whole business is retention.

**How a real server dies (failure scenario).** Week three, five-player server: three regulars, two weekenders. The regulars have restored nine decks, so the condition-weighted failure simulator now fills every evening with alarm triage — the session IS the Manifest, and "strategic responses" have collapsed into patch-prune-resplice rotation. The weekenders log in Saturday to find Deck 11 (and the tram loop they built) was jettisoned Tuesday while they were "in cryo," and an inspector drone classified their half-finished side-base as debris and tidied it into salvage; they churn immediately. That night a regular does the math on the Ledger: the reactor's remaining joules cannot reach the Burn at current draw no matter what they do — and separately notices the ship demonstrably fares better when nobody is online, since decay slows, the event budget shrinks, and no warm bodies bait the Verdance. The rational move is to stop playing; the server goes quiet; the diegetic anti-death-spiral logic ensures the quiet server is perfectly stable, i.e., the game ends not with a bang but with an equilibrium.

**The steelman fix.** Decouple doom from the wall clock: make The Dimming advance on simulation attention (player-hours and burn decisions, not real time) and cap event-budget scaling to restored territory only, never to online headcount. This one change preserves constant pressure and the sacrifice economy while eliminating both fatal emergent strategies — "the save expires whether we play or not" and "logging off is the best defense" — and it makes the persistent-server promise honest instead of tuned theater.

**Distinctiveness (4/5).** Closest game: Barotrauma. Shares the co-op maintain-a-dying-vessel-in-2D-dread loop, but Husklight swaps piloting and mission structure for persistent automation-building inside found architecture — a genuinely different core verb, so the difference matters even though every individual piece (Factorio logistics, ONI atmospherics, RimWorld decay) exists in shipped games.

**Feasibility (2/5).** The most expensive part is the always-on world simulation — coupled power/thermal/atmosphere networks, agent-based Custodians, and Verdance growth across a megastructure — which is the game's identity and therefore cannot be LOD-frozen without falsifying the 'ship keeps living while you're elsewhere' promise the pitch itself flags. Stacked on top: a procedural generator and an Inspection Protocol grader that must share a formal, machine-checkable architectural grammar, which is a research project, not a feature. Top-down 2D art and 20-player netcode are the only cheap line items.
