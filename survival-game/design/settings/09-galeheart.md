# 9. GALEHEART (working title: "Galeheart: Above the Churn")

> Seed: `sky` · Goal fit **32/40** · Distinctiveness **4/5** · Feasibility **2/5** · Verdict: **promising**

**Pitch.** A co-op survival-automation game on flying islands that are always sinking: every machine you bolt down adds weight, every watt you generate attracts the storm, and the sea below (the Churn) is waiting. You claw from hand-cranked dynamos to wind-shaft factories, lightning farms, and kite-borne logistics — until your automation matures enough that you stop chasing the drifting map and start steering your entire island across it.

**The hook.** A factory game where every machine you bolt down weighs your flying island toward the drowning sea — until your automation matures enough that you stop chasing the storm-shuffled map and sail your entire base across it.

**Tone.** Painterly isometric 2.5D — Bastion's floating shards meets Moebius sky palettes: hand-inked rigging and copper machines, flat-shaded characters, and wind rendered as visible streaking particle-lines (which doubles as a gameplay readout, keeping art cost honest for a small browser team). Emotionally: cozy lantern-lit decks against an enormous indifferent storm — precarious, awed, melancholy-hopeful.

## How it serves the founding goals

### Goal 1 — constant pressure

Three interlocking pressures, none of which expire. (1) THE SINK: your island floats on a keelstone whose charge constantly decays; the gauge (the "Ledger of Lift") is always draining. Low charge = lower altitude, and altitude IS safety: descend and Churn-spray corrodes your structures, churnbeasts board from below, and wind (your power source) weakens. Crucially, every structure adds ballast, permanently raising your charge upkeep — so your own progress raises your survival bill. (2) THE GALES: semi-predictable storm fronts (readable on an in-game barometer, ~20-40 min cadence) that tear sails, hurl lightning, and reshuffle island drift. Gales scale with YOU, not with a timer: charge radiance from your industry draws bigger storms and storm-fauna (static jellies, thunder wyrms), so a 100-hour base faces hurricanes a starter camp never sees. (3) THE RUST TAX: corrosion and windthrow degrade everything, demanding repair material forever. Pressure stays interesting rather than chore-like because every pressure is also an opportunity with a live decision attached: a gale is your best lightning harvest and fastest travel wind (raise rods and ride it, or furl and hide?); descending toward the Churn unlocks the richest fishing and salvage (skim low deliberately?); even the sink can be weaponized as a "descent burn" to steer. You are never grinding to hold a status quo — you are always choosing which danger to monetize.

### Goal 2 — the automation layers

Tier-0 manual life: hand-cranking the keelstone dynamo, hand-carrying baskets, hand-smelting copper scrap at a coal brazier, hand-repairing corrosion, hand-fishing stormglass off cliff edges, personally gliding cargo between islands. Six automation layers replace it: (L1) WIND-SHAFT WORKS — windmills, camshafts, gust-lines, and cable-trolley baskets automate hauling and processing (saws, threshers, ore stamps); they only run when air flows across them, so placement and terrain shape output. (L2) THE CHARGE GRID — stormglass conduits, capacitor banks, lightning rods, galvanic refiners, and arc-forges automate cranking and smelting; conduits leak on sloppy runs and arc when overloaded. (L3) AERIAL ROUTES — courier kites, cargo balloons, and auto-docks fly fixed routes down wind-lanes between islands, ending your life as a pack mule; wind-lanes shift after gales, so routes need re-rigging. (L4) SPRITE CREWS — spark-wisps captured in combat (Leyden cages) become bound workers that patch corrosion, reload harpoon racks, and tend skyroot crops: the maintenance-chore killer. (L5) STORM-LOGIC — barometric switches, storm-vanes, and semaphore lines automate gale response (auto-furl sails, auto-raise rods, auto-seal hatches), converting panic minutes into engineering. (L6) THE HELM — trim-sails, drift anchors, and a salvaged rudder assembly let you steer the island itself: the endgame automates reachability — instead of chasing drifting resources, your base sails to them. Why it's fresh vs Factorio's belts: power and logistics are DIRECTIONAL and WEATHER-DEPENDENT (you design for a wind envelope, not a fixed throughput), the logistics graph itself keeps changing, everything you automate has mass that costs lift, and the pinnacle isn't a bigger factory — it's a factory that sails.

### Goal 2 — the sacrifice economy

Sacrifice is literal physics, not a menu tax. CHARGE: capacitor power either feeds the keelstone (altitude, safety) or your machines — running the arc-forge for an hour visibly lowers your island into churnbeast altitude. MASS: every machine adds ballast, permanently raising upkeep — each build is a bet that it out-earns its own weight. EXPOSURE: the best materials (fulgurite, wyrm galls, skysilver) are harvested during gales at the island rim, trading body-safety for capability; rim-placed rods and buoys perform best and die first. TERRITORY: claiming outposts multiplies income and multiplies what drift and raiders can take from you. Worked decision, MINUTE 30: keelstone at 40% and falling, gale ten minutes out, you hold 12 copper scrap. (a) Hand-crank now — safe, dull, eats the ten minutes; (b) spend the copper on a first lightning rod + capacitor to harvest the strike — powered for an hour if placed well, grain shed burns if not; (c) build a wind-crank — passive charging forever, but slow, and you shelter through the gale losing your standing skyroot to windthrow. Worked decision, HOUR 20: the drift chart says Foundry Isle — your skysilver outpost, fed by two kite routes — exits kite range about two hours after the next gale. (a) Burn 60% of your capacitor bank on a descent-steer to chase it, spending a night at boarding altitude (your defenses finally earn their cost); (b) strip the outpost and eat the infrastructure loss; (c) spend the skysilver you'd saved for the arc-forge upgrade on relay buoys to stretch the route; (d) release it and re-plan production around the fulgurite reef the drift is bringing instead — energy-rich, food-poor. It hurts well because every option converts one kind of security into another; there is no "just wait."

### Goal 4 — the map changes the plan

The generator varies: starting-island biome and mass, prevailing wind belt, storm-corridor proximity, altitude band, and the drift orbits of your neighbors (which islands will pass within reach, and when). Examples: (1) Dealt an IRON CARCASS ISLE — metal everywhere, catastrophically heavy, sinking fast — the island becomes a launchpad, not a home: industrialize dirty, accept the descent, and race to build an airship and migrate before it drowns. (2) Dealt a BONEWOOD ATOLL in a high-wind belt — light, buoyant, flammable, metal-poor — you go all-in on sail power with almost no charge grid; low radiance means small storms find you, so you play quiet and get metal by raiding wrecks and trading, not mining. (3) Spawned inside THE ALLEY (storm corridor) — gales weekly-in-game — long kite routes are a sucker's bet, so you skip layer 3, overinvest in lightning harvest and storm-logic, and use the constant drift reshuffles themselves as your transport system, riding new adjacencies instead of building permanent ones. Drift means the map keeps re-dealing mid-game, so reactivity isn't just a spawn-time flavor choice.

### Goal 5 — base ⇄ combat loop

Base → combat: (1) your charge grid IS your ammunition — arc-lances and tesla bulwarks fire straight off capacitor banks, so grid engineering equals sustained firepower, and a mid-fight brownout is a base-design failure you can fix; (2) the refinery chain (galvanic refiner → arc-forge) yields skysilver harpoon heads and insulated storm-mail that let you fight DURING gales instead of hiding from them; (3) glider/airship workshop quality sets combat mobility — dive attacks from altitude, boarding a thunder wyrm mid-flight. Combat/exploration → base: (1) thunder wyrm galls are living capacitors, the best energy storage in the game, kill-only; (2) spark-wisps captured alive (not killed) become sprite workers — automation layer 4 is a combat-capture economy; (3) storm-cult raider wrecks yield hull ribs and helm charts that unlock airship frames and the island rudder; (4) clearing a churnbeast nest off a derelict islet lets you claim it as an outrigger annex — territory as loot. Raiders complete the loop with stakes: NPC cult skiffs come to siphon keelstone charge — they steal your survival resource, so defense is never optional decoration.

### Goals 3 + 6 — many paths, no milestones

No research tree. Two systems replace it. (1) SCHEMA-BY-SALVAGE: blueprints are learned by studying real machines in the world — wrecks, drowned ruins, raider gear, even crewmates' builds — at a drafting table. The world is the tech tree, and the procedural map (plus drift) decides which branches are near you this run, in what order. (2) PHYSICS GATES, NOT TIER FLAGS: an arc-forge doesn't need "Tier 3" — it needs 40 sustained amps and 200 lift-units of headroom, and you can get there via a wyrm-gall bank, a capacitor farm, a lucky lightning corridor, or docking beside an energy-rich neighbor. Progression is continuous quantities — peak amps, lift headroom, route reach, forecast horizon — so there is no moment the game stamps "milestone." Genuinely different viable archetypes: THE SPIRE (one tall fortified island tuned for maximum lightning catch; huge storms, deep defense, trades for what it can't make); THE FLOTILLA (near-zero home mass, life aboard airships and towed buoy-platforms, chasing resource islands and dodging storms rather than tanking them); THE WEB (many light single-purpose islets linked by kite routes — resilient to losing any node, ruinously exposed to drift reshuffles, so it overinvests in drift anchors and forecast towers); THE SKIMMER (deliberately run low over the Churn where salvage and fishing are rich and storms can't reach, tanking corrosion with sprite repair crews).

### Goal 7 — beauty pays rent

Three physical systems make beauty load-bearing, all readable through one overlay (the "streamline lens"). (1) FLOW: a coarse 2D wind field crosses your island; buildings cast turbulence wakes. Turbines want laminar feed, forges want channelled draft, crops and docks want sheltered calm — so optimal bases grow curved windbreak walls, staggered roof heights, aligned sail-lines, and quiet interior courtyards: the exact vocabulary of builds that photograph well, arrived at for output reasons. (2) TRIM: mass distribution tilts the island; a listing island spills cisterns, stresses joints, and starves lee-side turbines — so balance and considered symmetry are throughput, not taste. (3) ROD RHYTHM: lightning rods protect a radius; cramming structures into a dense blob exceeds coverage and gets something struck every gale — so spacing and cadence are survival. It isn't a bolted-on beauty meter because there is no beauty score at all: the wind, the tilt, and the lightning simply reward the same shapes human eyes do, and the streamline lens makes tuning your island feel like voicing an instrument.

### Goal 8 — why you're doing this

Frame: the world was once whole; the Aeon Gale shattered it, and whatever loses charge drowns in the Churn. You are Keepers of a keelstone — a still-living fragment of the old world's heart that remembers what drowned. Outer goal: raise something back — accumulate enough sustained lift and charge to RAISE DROWNED RUINS from the Churn, each raising being a crew-chosen mega-project that permanently rewrites the local map (a new island, new biome, new salvageable schemas — and bigger storms drawn to it), culminating, if a server wants it, in raising a Landfall Beacon. Not a quest chain: which ruins, what order, or whether you go nomad and never raise anything is entirely emergent. Four mechanisms cascade from that frame to the current second: (1) the LEDGER OF LIFT — the keelstone gauge makes the fiction an instrument; every crank, wire, and harvest visibly feeds the stone that keeps you above the drown; (2) the BAROMETER — every session has a next-gale deadline generating "what must happen before it hits"; (3) the DRIFT CHART — the week's goals fall out of who is drifting toward or away from you: chase, anchor, or release; (4) STONE-MEMORIES — relics and charge thresholds trigger unordered memory-visions that deliver lore and point, as bearings and rumors rather than quest markers, toward raisable ruins and rare schemas. Chain of "why": I crank to feed the stone → the stone keeps us above the Churn and remembers → remembering shows what can be raised → raising puts land back under the sky.

## Multiplayer (2–20, persistent)

2-20 on a persistent server, one or more crews sharing keelstones — a shared drain-gauge is natural co-op glue, and the setting hands out roles without classes: helm/navigator (drift charts, steering, descent burns), stormwright (grid, rods, storm-logic), rigger (kites, routes, airships), warden (defense, wisp-capture), quartermaster (food, rust tax). The Web archetype lets individual players own personal islets inside a crew federation. Offline: an unmanned island can be BATTENED DOWN — the stone sleeps (decay slows to a trickle), storms do only cosmetic damage, but all production halts — or left RUNNING, where automation keeps producing and gales and raiders are real, so what you wake up to is a direct referendum on your storm-logic and defenses; that choice makes automation quality matter overnight and blunts (not erases) the second-job problem. Pressure scales with population organically: charge radiance sums across the server's industry, so bigger crews draw bigger gales; raider cult strength tracks server net worth; keelstone upkeep grows gently with claimed islands per capita so 20 players can't trivialize the sink.

## Closest existing games

- Factorio — belts on static land vs. wind-and-charge logistics over a drifting island graph with a weight budget
- Raft — drifting survival, but with industrial automation depth and a steerable base
- Airborne Kingdom — flying city aesthetic, but with survival teeth, combat, and co-op
- Don't Starve Together — persistent co-op pressure and seasons, but automation-first rather than hand-to-mouth forever
- Nova Lands — island-hopping automation, but static, safe, and single-player where Galeheart is hostile, drifting, and shared

## Designer-admitted risks

- Simulation legibility and server cost: coarse wind-flow, drift orbits, and per-island charge simulated server-side for 20 browser clients is a real perf budget, and if the flow field feels mushy ('why isn't my turbine spinning?') the aesthetics mechanic collapses — the streamline lens has to be genuinely excellent UX, not a debug view.
- Drift reshuffles destroy logistics investment by design; the line between delicious pressure and rage-quit is thin and lives entirely in tuning (forecast generosity, salvage refunds, relay-buoy costs). Get it wrong one way and drift is noise, the other way and nobody builds layer-3 routes at all.
- The charge-attracts-storms feedback loop can wall off ambition: if growth is strictly punished, the game becomes anti-Factorio feel-bad. Needs a sub-linear radiance curve plus counterplay (grounding arrays, decoy spires) — and the mass/lift budget has the same failure mode if lift ceilings don't scale generously.
- No tech tree means no map of what's possible: schema-by-salvage risks illegibility and dead-end seeds if the generator distributes discoverable machines poorly; players may need a soft 'rumor compass' that flirts with becoming the quest log the design forbids.
- Mixed-schedule groups still face obligation dynamics: batten-down softens the Rust/ARK second-job problem, but the shared keelstone means one hardcore player can become the crew's unpaid night watchman, and the social design around that is unsolved.

**Biggest weakness.** The economy is anti-growth on three stacked axes: every machine permanently raises survival upkeep, industry output attracts bigger storms, and drift destroys logistics investment by design — so the genre's core compulsion loop (build more, feel more powerful) is mathematically inverted into 'build more, owe more.' The pitch's own risks section concedes each knife-edge individually but never confronts that it has stacked all three, and it offers no demonstrated tuning point where sacrifice stays meaningful without making deconstruction the optimal response to any setback. A survival-automation game where tearing your factory apart is frequently the correct move is fighting its own genre.

**How a real server dies (failure scenario).** Hour 16, three friends on a persistent server. They left the island RUNNING overnight to keep the refiners fed; radiance from that industry pulled a hurricane-class gale before their storm-logic (L5) was built, and a cult skiff siphoned the keelstone during it. They log in to 22% charge, rust everywhere, and a lee-side turbine row dead because storm damage tilted the island. The entire session becomes tier-0 regression: hand-cranking, hand-patching, hand-hauling — the exact chores they spent fifteen hours automating — and they cannot build their way out because every new structure raises the very upkeep that is drowning them. Mid-session the drift chart announces their skysilver outpost is leaving kite range, severing both routes they spent four hours rigging. The spreadsheet-minded player computes that the highest-EV move is to scrap the arc-forge and two windmills to shed ballast; they dismantle a third of their factory to buy altitude, someone says 'so the game punishes us for playing it,' and nobody logs back in. The batten-down alternative would have avoided this only by making the world stop mattering — which is the other way the game dies.

**The steelman fix.** Move mass off the survival ledger and onto the spatial ledger: ballast should tax trim, tilt, steering agility, and drift speed (where you put weight, how nimble your island is) rather than permanently raising keelstone drain — and cap storm scaling to time-and-territory instead of watts, so growth is never strictly self-taxing. Constant pressure then comes from external clocks that don't compound with player progress (gale cadence, drift reshuffles, rust), preserving goals 1 and 2 while deleting the deconstruction death spiral, which is the one place this design collapses into anti-Factorio feel-bad.

**Distinctiveness (4/5).** Closest game: Raft. Raft already ships 'drifting co-op survival on a base you eventually steer,' but nothing shipped combines that with Factorio-depth automation under a weight budget on a wind-driven island graph — the recombination is genuinely unoccupied space, though every constituent verb exists elsewhere, so distinctiveness lives in the systems integration actually working, not in any single new idea.

**Feasibility (2/5).** The expensive core is server-authoritative coupled simulation: a wind flow field that recomputes legibly on every building placement, island trim physics, drift orbits across a persistent world map, per-island charge grids, and offline raid/production resolution — all synced to up to 20 browser clients in real time, and inseparable from the UX claims because if the streamline lens feels mushy, goal 7 dies outright. This is effectively three games (an automation sim, a survival-combat game, and a fleet-navigation layer) plus six interlocking automation tiers; a small team ships it only by cutting at least one pillar, and the pitch identifies none as cuttable.
